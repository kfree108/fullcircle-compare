How to Become an Amazon Reviewer: What Actually Works

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1248 words · Written against what currently ranked for “become an amazon reviewer”
The short answer

There are two legitimate paths. Amazon Vine invites you based on your existing review history — no application exists, and no one is paid. Amazon Associates lets you apply as a content creator and earn commission on sales, not on reviews. There is no third, paid 'professional reviewer' role on Amazon.

The Two Real Paths, and the One That Doesn't Exist

Search results and forum posts blur these together, so start here: Amazon Vine gets you free products in exchange for honest reviews. Amazon Associates gets you a commission when someone buys through your link, whether or not you ever call yourself a reviewer. They are separate programs with separate rules, and you can be in both at once.

What doesn't exist is a job called 'Amazon reviewer' that pays a wage or a per-review fee. If a site, course, or Facebook group is charging you to 'get you approved' for Vine, or promising paid review gigs direct from Amazon, that's not a real program. Amazon doesn't sell access to either one.

Amazon Vine: How the Invite Actually Works

There is no application form for Vine. Amazon's own page says it plainly: Voices are selected because they've 'consistently written insightful reviews,' and the invite comes to you. You cannot request one, pay for one, or speed it up by writing more reviews in a short burst — Amazon is watching for a pattern of useful, detailed reviews over time, not volume.

Once invited, you request items from a rotating catalog, they arrive free, and you review them. The review carries a badge disclosing it was a free Vine item. You aren't paid, and you're expected to leave the review you'd actually give — positive, neutral, or negative. Voices who stop reviewing honestly, or who only leave five-star reviews regardless of the product, tend to lose access.

If you've been reviewing on Amazon for months and haven't been invited: keep writing reviews that explain how you actually used the product and what went wrong, not just that you liked it. That's the signal Amazon is filtering for. There's nothing else to check or fix — this one runs on Amazon's own criteria, not yours.

Amazon Associates: Applying as a Reviewer or Creator

This is the path if you want to earn money reviewing products. You need a platform first — a website, YouTube channel, or social account with real followers — then you apply. Amazon's own requirements are specific: your site needs original content, generally at least 10 posts, updated within the last 60 days, and it can't sit behind a paywall or in a closed group. Social applications need roughly 500 organic followers on a public page.

After you apply, Amazon checks your site and waits for activity: you need at least three qualifying sales through your tagged links within 180 days. Orders you place yourself don't count, even if you genuinely bought the product to review it.

  • Website reviewers: own the domain, publish real reviews, embed tagged links correctly.
  • Social/video reviewers: public page, visible follower count, platform limited to Facebook, Instagram, Twitter/X, YouTube, TikTok, and Twitch.
  • Everyone: three tracked sales in 180 days, from someone other than you.

A Worked Example: What 'Three Qualifying Sales' Actually Looks Like

Say you run a home-goods blog and publish a review of a $40 kitchen tool with your tagged link embedded. Three readers click through and buy it within Amazon's tracking window — that's your three qualifying sales, and your application clears review. The commission itself is a percentage of the sale price, and that percentage varies by product category rather than being one flat number, so don't build your math around a single figure you saw quoted somewhere.

The part people miss: those three sales have to come from someone else's click. Buying the item yourself through your own link to 'test if the tag works' doesn't count, and doing it repeatedly can read as exactly the kind of self-generated activity the program is built to screen out.

The Mistakes That Get You Rejected or Stuck

Most rejections trace back to a handful of things:

  • Applying to Associates with thin content. A site with three posts and a lot of affiliate links looks like a link farm, not a review site, and gets rejected on originality grounds.
  • Closed groups or hidden follower counts. If Amazon's reviewer can't see your audience, they reject the application — they won't take your word for it.
  • Counting your own orders. Buying through your own link to hit the '3 sales' threshold doesn't qualify and can flag the account.
  • Assuming Vine has a queue you can join. There's no waitlist, no form, no fee that gets you in faster. Any service claiming otherwise is not Amazon.
  • Incentivizing clicks. Telling your audience 'buy through my link to support me' violates the Associates operating agreement outright — it has to be disclosed as an affiliate link, not framed as a favor.

Which one you should actually pick

Vine suits people who already review honestly and often and don't need it to pay — the reward is free products, not income. Associates suits people who already have an audience and want to monetize content they'd be making anyway. Neither is a paid reviewing job, and anyone selling you a shortcut into either one isn't Amazon.

Common questions

How long does it take to get invited to Amazon Vine?

There's no published timeline, and Amazon doesn't give a reason if it hasn't happened yet. It's based on a pattern of consistently useful reviews built up over time, not a switch that flips after a fixed number of reviews or days.

Can I pay someone to get me into Amazon Vine faster?

No. Any service selling Vine access or guaranteed invites is not affiliated with Amazon and isn't offering a real product — Vine has no application, so there's nothing legitimate to sell.

Does Amazon Associates pay me to write reviews?

No. It pays commission when someone buys through your tagged link. Writing a great review that nobody clicks through and buys from earns nothing; a mediocre review with a link that converts does.

My Associates application was rejected — what do I check first?

Check three things in order: whether your site or social page is fully public with no closed groups, whether you have at least 10 original posts published in the last 60 days, and whether any of your 'qualifying' sales were actually your own orders, which don't count.

Can I be a Vine Voice and an Amazon Associate at the same time?

Yes. They're run independently — Vine reviews products from your own past behavior as a shopper, Associates pays commission on your content's referral traffic. Holding both isn't restricted either way.
