A+ Content in Vendor Central: What's Different From Seller Central

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1415 words · Written against what currently ranked for “vendor central a+ content”
The short answer

Vendor Central A+ Content uses the same module library as Seller Central but a different gate: vendors don't need Brand Registry, because Vendor Central access is itself the credential. The catalogue sits behind Amazon's purchase-order system rather than a seller's own inventory feed, which changes how and when A+ edits actually go live.

The gate is different, not the content

On Seller Central, A+ Content is locked behind Brand Registry — a trademark, an application, an approval. On Vendor Central, that gate doesn't exist in the same form: being invited into Vendor Central and holding a vendor code is Amazon's own verification that you're the manufacturer or authorised distributor of the brand, so the trademark-and-registry step that a 3P seller goes through has already happened at onboarding. The module library itself — image headers, comparison charts, brand story, the Premium set with video and hotspots — is the same set of templates either side uses. What differs is the path to unlock it, and in Vendor Central's case, a separate account manager relationship that sometimes gates Premium access rather than a self-serve eligibility page.

A+ Content sits on top of a PO-driven catalogue

The bigger practical difference isn't the content tool, it's what it's attached to. A Seller Central listing is inventory the seller controls directly — list it, price it, ship it. A Vendor Central catalogue item exists because Amazon issues purchase orders against it; the vendor doesn't set retail price (Amazon does, and can discount below the vendor's cost basis to win the Buy Box), and the ASIN can go out of stock for reasons that have nothing to do with the vendor's own warehouse — a PO Amazon simply didn't place that week. A+ Content changes the presentation of the page; it does nothing to the purchase order pipeline underneath it, and vendors who treat an A+ refresh as a fix for a stock or pricing problem are solving the wrong layer.

A worked example: the same module, two different failure modes

A comparison-chart module showing three SKU variants side by side works identically in both systems when it's live. Where it breaks differs. On Seller Central, the chart goes dark if the seller lets one of the three ASINs go out of stock in their own fulfilment network — a supply problem the seller directly controls and can usually fix within days. On Vendor Central, the same chart goes dark if Amazon simply stops issuing purchase orders for one variant, which can happen on a vendor's high-performing SKU as easily as a slow one, driven by Amazon's own inventory planning rather than anything the brand did. The fix in the second case isn't a catalogue edit — it's a conversation with the vendor manager about forecast and PO cadence, which A+ Content has no lever over.

Where vendors lose Premium eligibility without noticing

Premium A+ Content's eligibility criteria — an approval history, a published Brand Story across owned ASINs — get checked per brand, and a vendor account with a large but inconsistently maintained catalogue can lose that eligibility quietly. If a subset of ASINs under the brand have no A+ Content at all, or the Brand Story module isn't published account-wide, Premium access can be pulled or withheld without a dramatic notification — it just stops appearing as an option the next time someone opens the content manager. The practical fix is treating A+ coverage as a catalogue-wide maintenance task, not a project you complete once and leave.

This matters more on Vendor Central than Seller Central because vendor catalogues tend to be older and larger — brands that have sold wholesale to Amazon for years often have discontinued or low-priority ASINs sitting in the catalogue with no A+ Content at all, dragging down the brand-wide coverage percentage that Premium eligibility is checked against. Auditing coverage across the full ASIN list, not just the SKUs currently being actively managed, is the fix most vendors skip.

Who actually has edit access, and why that slows vendors down

Seller Central A+ Content is usually built and published by whoever holds account access — often one person or a small in-house team with direct login. Vendor Central accounts more often route through a vendor manager relationship and sometimes a separate merchandising or category team on Amazon's side, particularly for larger vendors. That extra layer isn't a formal approval requirement in the content tool itself, but it changes the practical timeline: a vendor waiting on a vendor-manager conversation to unlock Premium eligibility is on a different clock than a seller checking a self-serve eligibility page inside their own dashboard. Budget for that when planning a content refresh around a launch date — the design work takes the same number of days either side, but the access and eligibility conversation on the vendor side can add weeks.

The mistake vendors make more than sellers do

Because Vendor Central doesn't gate A+ Content behind an application the way Brand Registry does, brands sometimes assume the content itself carries less compliance risk on the vendor side — that Amazon's own first-party relationship means more latitude on claims and comparison language. It doesn't. The same restrictions on subjective claims, competitor mentions and promotional pricing language apply identically; the review queue is separate but the standard isn't lower. We've watched vendor-side A+ modules get rejected for language that would have been rejected on Seller Central too, on brands that assumed the vendor relationship bought them more room.

Where an agency actually adds value here

The A+ Content design work is genuinely the same skill either side of the Vendor Central line. What differs is diagnosing whether a listing problem is content, price, or PO cadence — and that diagnosis requires someone who can see the purchase order pattern, not just the product page. Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon spend across 100+ brands, including hybrid catalogues like HexClad and Ridge that run Vendor Central for some retail relationships and Seller Central for direct control — and the recurring fix on the vendor side is exactly this: an A+ refresh that looked like the answer, when the real blocker was a PO gap the vendor manager relationship needed to solve.

Which one you should actually pick

If your catalogue is Vendor Central only, the design work is identical to Seller Central A+ Content — build it the same way. The thing worth watching is coverage across the whole brand, since losing Premium eligibility on Vendor Central can happen quietly through gaps in Brand Story or approval history that nobody's actively tracking. If a listing problem looks like it needs an A+ refresh, check the PO pattern first.

Common questions

Do vendors need Brand Registry for A+ Content?

No. Vendor Central access itself functions as the credential Brand Registry provides on the seller side — a vendor account already establishes the manufacturer or authorised-distributor relationship that a 3P seller has to separately prove.

Can A+ Content fix a Vendor Central stock problem?

No. A+ Content changes the page presentation; it has no connection to Amazon's purchase order pipeline. If an ASIN is out of stock because Amazon hasn't issued a PO for it, refreshing the A+ modules does nothing to the underlying availability.

Is Premium A+ Content harder to get on Vendor Central?

Not necessarily harder, but the path can be less self-serve — eligibility is still checked per brand against an approval history and Brand Story coverage, and on the vendor side that sometimes runs through the vendor manager relationship rather than a self-service eligibility page.

Does Amazon review vendor A+ Content less strictly?

No. The same restrictions on unsupported claims, competitor mentions and promotional language apply on both sides. The review queue is separate; the standard isn't lower, and vendor-side submissions get rejected for the same reasons seller-side ones do.
