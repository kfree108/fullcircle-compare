Is the Amazon Credit Card Actually Good? Here's the Math

The short answer

Yes, if you're a Prime member who pays the balance in full every month. You get 5% back at Amazon and Whole Foods with no annual fee. Without Prime, or if you carry a balance, the math gets a lot less impressive fast.

There are two cards, not one

Chase issues two versions of this card, and most reviews blur them together. The Prime Visa is for people with an eligible Prime membership: 5% back at Amazon.com, Whole Foods Market, and on Chase Travel purchases, 2% back on gas, restaurants, and local transit, and 1% on everything else. New cardmembers get a $150 Amazon gift card on approval.

The Amazon Visa is the non-Prime version. Same structure, lower rate: 3% instead of 5% on Amazon, Whole Foods, and Chase Travel. Signup bonus is $50 instead of $150. Neither card has an annual fee, and neither charges foreign transaction fees. You cannot hold both at once — Chase requires you to close one before opening the other.

Run the math on your own spending, not the average

Here's a worked example using a fairly ordinary spending pattern: $150/month at Amazon.com, $100/month at Whole Foods, $200/month combined on gas, restaurants and transit, and $550/month on everything else. That's $12,000 a year in card spend.

As a Prime member: $90/year from Amazon (5%), $60 from Whole Foods (5%), $48 from gas/restaurants/transit (2%), $66 from everything else (1%) — $264/year in rewards, plus a $150 signup bonus.

Without Prime, on the Amazon Visa: $54 + $36 + $48 + $66 = $204/year, plus a $50 signup bonus. The gap between the two cards on this spend profile is $60/year in ongoing rewards and $100 upfront — not nothing, but not the whole story either. Someone who barely shops at Amazon or Whole Foods will see almost no difference between the two cards, because the categories where they diverge (Amazon, Whole Foods, Chase Travel) are the only ones where the rate changes.

Where people get this wrong

The rewards rate is real. The mistake is comparing it against the wrong thing. Chase publishes a variable APR of 18.74%–27.49% on this card. Carry $1,000 on the card for a year at the high end of that range and you owe roughly $275 in interest — more than the entire $264/year in rewards from the example above. The card doesn't become bad at that point. The math just moves entirely to the interest line and the rewards line stops mattering.

The second mistake is treating the 10%+ rotating category bonus as part of the baseline math. It's real, but it's limited-time and applies to a rotating selection of items, not a fixed category you can plan around. Build your comparison on the guaranteed 3%/5%/2%/1% structure, and treat the rotating bonus as a bonus, not baseline.

The third: applying for the 0% promo APR on a purchase over $250 without checking what happens after the promo window. It's 0% for a fixed period, then the standard 18.74%–27.49% variable rate applies to whatever's left. That's a fine tool for a planned purchase you'll pay off on schedule. It's a bad tool for anything open-ended.

When this card isn't the right one

If you don't pay the statement in full most months, the APR range makes this a poor fit regardless of the rewards rate — that's true of almost every rewards card, not a knock on this one specifically. If you travel by air often, the 3%/5% only applies to purchases made through Chase Travel directly; it doesn't touch airline miles, seat upgrades, or lounge access the way a dedicated travel card does. And if your spending is mostly outside Amazon, Whole Foods, gas, restaurants, and transit, the 1% catch-all rate is unremarkable next to flat 2% cash-back cards with no category restrictions.

Where it's genuinely strong: someone already paying for Prime, who does real grocery and shopping volume at Amazon and Whole Foods, and who clears the balance every month. That's a specific person, not everyone with an Amazon account.

Which one you should actually pick

Good for a Prime member who shops Amazon and Whole Foods regularly and pays the statement in full — the 5% rate and $150 bonus are genuinely strong for that person. Weak for anyone who carries a balance, travels by air often, or spends mostly outside Amazon's ecosystem; the APR or the flat 1% rate erodes the case fast.

Common questions

Does having this credit card affect my Amazon seller account?

No. The Prime Visa and Amazon Visa are personal consumer credit products issued by Chase. They have no connection to a seller account, advertising account, or brand's standing on Amazon. Separate systems entirely.

Should I get the Prime Visa or the Amazon Visa?

If you already pay for Prime, the Prime Visa's 5% rate on Amazon and Whole Foods almost always beats the Amazon Visa's 3%, and the signup bonus is three times larger. If you don't have Prime and aren't planning to get it, the Amazon Visa gives you the same no-annual-fee structure without paying for a membership you wouldn't otherwise use.

Is the 10% back on rotating categories worth chasing?

It's worth taking when it lines up with something you were already buying. It's not worth changing your shopping around, because it applies to a limited, rotating selection of items and is explicitly time-limited. Treat the guaranteed 3%/5%/2%/1% rates as the real value and the rotating bonus as a bonus.

What happens if I carry a balance instead of paying in full?

The variable APR range on this card is 18.74%–27.49%. On a $1,000 balance carried for a year, that's roughly $190–$275 in interest — more than most people earn back in rewards on that spending. If you regularly carry a balance, the rewards rate stops being the relevant number.

Can I have both the Prime Visa and the Amazon Visa?

No. Chase's own terms state the card is available only if you don't currently hold the other version. You'd need to close one before opening the other.
