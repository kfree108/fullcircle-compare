Amazon Purchasing Policy Violation: There's No Single Document by That Name

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1410 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon purchasing policy violation”
The short answer

Amazon doesn't publish one policy called "the Purchasing Policy." A notice using that phrase almost always maps to one of a few specific rules — product sourcing and authenticity requirements inside the Seller Code of Conduct, restricted-product purchasing rules, or a supplier-invoice requirement tied to an ungating request — and the fix depends on identifying which specific rule actually applies.

The honest starting point: this isn't one named policy

Searching Amazon's own published documentation for a single page titled "Purchasing Policy" doesn't turn up one. What exists instead is a set of related rules spread across several documents: the Selling Policies and Seller Code of Conduct, which covers where and how inventory is sourced and what documentation proves it's authentic; category-specific restricted-product rules, which govern what a seller can source and list at all; and ungating requirements, which ask for supplier invoices as proof of a legitimate sourcing chain before certain categories or brands unlock. A seller receiving a notice that references "purchasing" language is almost certainly hitting one of these three, not a fourth document that doesn't exist.

This is worth saying plainly rather than working around: if you cannot find a policy page, the honest response is describing what the notice is actually asking for based on what the underlying rule requires, not inventing a citation that sounds more official than what genuinely exists.

Sourcing and authenticity: the Seller Code of Conduct angle

The most common source of a "purchasing" notice is Amazon's authenticity and sourcing requirement: sellers must be able to demonstrate their inventory came through a legitimate supply chain, typically via invoices from an authorized distributor or manufacturer showing quantities consistent with what's being sold. This isn't phrased as a single "Purchasing Policy" inside Seller Central — it's part of the broader Code of Conduct's product authenticity and restricted-content sections, and it surfaces most often when a seller is asked to prove authenticity after a complaint, or during ungating for a gated category or brand.

Ungating: where 'purchasing' documentation actually gets checked hardest

Ungating a restricted category or a gated brand is where purchasing documentation gets scrutinized most directly. Amazon typically wants invoices from an authorized distributor, not a retailer — this distinction trips up a meaningful number of sellers, since a retail receipt (even from a large, reputable retailer) generally doesn't satisfy the requirement the way a distributor invoice does. Amazon is checking supply-chain legitimacy specifically, not just proof that money changed hands for the product.

The invoice itself typically needs to show enough volume to plausibly account for the quantity being sold — a single small invoice submitted to unlock a listing selling hundreds of units a month tends to raise a follow-up question rather than close the case, since it doesn't actually demonstrate the ongoing supply relationship the requirement is checking for.

A worked example: the notice that sent someone looking for a document that didn't exist

A seller receives a notice citing a "purchasing practices" concern after a customer complaint about product authenticity. Searching for "Amazon Purchasing Policy" directly turns up nothing official — which reads, incorrectly, like the notice references something that doesn't exist. What's actually being asked for is documented proof of the supply chain: invoices, in the right format, from an authorized source, tied to the specific ASIN in question. The notice's language is broader than the actual requirement, and the fix is locating the specific authenticity documentation requirement inside the Code of Conduct rather than searching for a policy page matching the notice's exact wording.

What to do when the notice's language doesn't match anything you can find

If a notice cites something you can't locate as a named policy, the reliable move is opening a support case and asking Amazon to specify the exact policy section and the exact documentation needed to resolve it — not guessing based on the notice's plain-language phrasing. Vague notices tend to reference a real, specific underlying rule using looser everyday language than the rule itself uses, and getting Amazon to name the section directly is faster than trying to reverse-engineer it from search.

The other place "purchasing" shows up: buyer-side quantity limits

There's a second, unrelated meaning worth naming so it doesn't get confused with the seller-side issue above: Amazon also limits how much of a given item a single customer account can purchase, particularly on high-demand or restricted items, and a buyer account that trips this limit sees a different kind of "purchasing" restriction entirely — a checkout-side quantity block, not a seller policy notice. If the phrase "purchasing policy" appears in a context about buying rather than selling, it's this consumer-side limit, and it has no connection to the seller sourcing and authenticity requirements covered above. The two get confused often enough in casual search that it's worth ruling out which side of the transaction the notice you actually received is even about.

A second worked example: an ungating request denied for the wrong document

A seller applying to sell a gated brand submits a retail receipt from a large national retailer as proof of purchase, expecting it to satisfy the sourcing requirement — it doesn't, and the application is denied without much explanation beyond "insufficient documentation." The actual requirement was an invoice from an authorized distributor showing a wholesale relationship, which a retail receipt structurally can't demonstrate regardless of how legitimate the original purchase was. Resubmitting with distributor documentation, rather than a better copy of the same retail receipt, is what actually resolves this — the format of the proof matters as much as its existence, and knowing that before the first submission saves an entire review cycle most sellers only learn about after losing one.

Which one you should actually pick

Don't spend time searching for a policy page matching a notice's exact wording — ask Amazon directly which specific section it's citing. In practice, a 'purchasing' notice is almost always about proving a legitimate, authorized supply chain, and the fix is documentation from a distributor, not a general appeal or a receipt from wherever the inventory happened to be bought.

Common questions

Is there an official Amazon document called the 'Purchasing Policy'?

No single document by that exact name. The phrase in a notice usually maps to sourcing and authenticity requirements inside the Seller Code of Conduct, restricted-product rules, or ungating documentation requirements — the specific one depends on the actual notice.

What documentation does Amazon actually want for a purchasing-related notice?

Most commonly, invoices from an authorized distributor or manufacturer showing quantities consistent with what's being sold — proof of a legitimate supply chain, not just proof of purchase. A retail receipt often doesn't satisfy this even from a reputable retailer.

Why can't I find the exact policy my notice mentions?

Notices often use plain-language phrasing that's broader than the specific rule being cited. Rather than searching for the exact wording, open a support case and ask Amazon to name the specific policy section and documentation required — that's faster than reverse-engineering it from search.

Does a retail receipt count as purchasing documentation for ungating?

Usually not. Amazon's ungating documentation requirement is typically looking for an invoice from an authorized distributor or manufacturer showing a wholesale relationship, which a retail receipt structurally can't demonstrate even when the underlying purchase was completely legitimate.

Is a buyer-account purchase limit the same as a seller purchasing policy violation?

No — these are unrelated. A buyer-side purchase limit restricts how much of an item one customer account can buy at checkout. A seller-side purchasing notice is about sourcing and authenticity documentation. The word "purchasing" covers both but they involve completely different systems and audiences.
