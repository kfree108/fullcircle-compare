The short answer

For a brand, the only Amazon-sanctioned path to early reviews from designated reviewers is the Vine program — Amazon's own trusted "Vine Voice" reviewers, not a seller-chosen reviewer. Fees run $0 for up to 2 enrolled units, $75 for 3–10, and $200 for 11–30, per parent product, charged only after the first review publishes. Offering any incentive for a review outside Vine breaches Amazon's terms, with no exception.

What this page is actually about

Most search volume behind "Amazon product reviewer" is from people looking to become one — free or discounted products in exchange for a review. That's a different audience than this page is written for. If you're a brand or seller trying to get early reviews on a new listing, the only Amazon-run, terms-compliant path is Amazon Vine, and it works nothing like a seller directly recruiting or compensating a reviewer. Vine reviewers are selected and matched by Amazon itself, from its own pool of trusted Vine Voices, based on the quality of their existing published reviews — a seller doesn't choose, contact, or communicate with them directly at any point.

Eligibility and enrollment: what a brand actually needs

Vine requires a Professional selling account and a Brand Representative or Reseller role on a brand enrolled in Amazon Brand Registry, along with eligible FBA offers on the ASIN being enrolled. The product itself needs fewer than 30 reviews at the time of enrollment — Vine is built for new or newly-launched listings, not for propping up an established one. Adult, digital and bundled products aren't eligible. New sellers who enroll a brand in Brand Registry can also get a $200 credit specifically toward Vine, which is worth checking for on newer accounts before assuming the fee applies in full.

What it actually costs

Per Amazon's own published fee structure, enrollment is priced by how many units of a parent product are made available to Vine reviewers: $0 for up to 2 units, $75 for 3–10 units, and $200 for 11–30 units — 30 units is the maximum enrollment, and 30 reviews is the resulting hard cap on a single product. Critically, the fee isn't charged upfront: Amazon doesn't bill until 30 days after enrollment and only once the first review has actually published, and if no review publishes within 90 days of enrollment, the fee is waived entirely. That structure means the cost is genuinely tied to the program working, not to simply signing up.

What Vine does, and what it does not do, for a launch

Vine's actual mechanism is straightforward: it supplies a small, credible batch of reviews from reviewers Amazon itself has vetted, which helps a brand-new listing clear the psychological and algorithmic barrier of having zero social proof. Amazon states it can boost sales by an average of around 30% — that's Amazon's own published figure describing the category effect, not a promise for any individual product, and actual results vary by category, price point and product quality. What Vine does not do: guarantee positive reviews (Vine Voices write genuine, honest reviews and can be critical), replace organic review growth over time, or substitute for the product actually being good — a mediocre product enrolled in Vine gets mediocre, honest reviews from Vine Voices exactly as it would from anyone else.

A worked example: what enrolling 20 units actually involves

A brand launches a new kitchen product with zero reviews and enrolls 20 units in Vine — landing in the $200 fee tier. Vine Voices browsing the program request units that interest them; there's no way to select or steer which reviewers pick up the product. Reviews begin publishing over the following weeks, not all at once. The brand is billed $200 roughly 30 days after enrollment, once the first review is live — if the first review takes 45 days to appear, the bill follows accordingly, still tied to that first published review rather than a fixed calendar date. By the time the full batch has published, the listing has moved from zero social proof to a real, if still modest, review base to build organic reviews on top of.

What happens after the 30-review cap is reached

Once a product's Vine allocation is exhausted — whichever comes first between the unit count enrolled and the 30-review cap — the listing moves entirely onto organic review growth for anything beyond that. Vine doesn't re-open on the same ASIN once it's been used and the 30-review threshold has been crossed, so it functions specifically as a launch bridge, not an ongoing review-generation channel. Planning for that transition — organic order volume, product quality, and normal customer review behavior taking over from there — is part of using Vine well rather than treating it as a program to lean on indefinitely.

Timing it against a launch, not just enrolling early

Because Vine reviews publish gradually rather than all at once, and because the fee only triggers once the first review is live, enrollment timed too close to a launch date can mean the listing goes live with the review count still building rather than already in place. Enrolling several weeks ahead of a planned launch, where the timeline allows it, gives Vine's review flow more room to land before the moment traffic actually ramps up — a listing with a modest review base already visible converts differently than one launching at zero and hoping reviews catch up afterward.

The line that matters most: what's outside Vine is a policy violation

Anything that trades free product, payment, discounts or any other incentive for a review outside the Vine program — arranged directly with a reviewer, a coordinated batch of reviewers, or any similar structure — breaches Amazon's terms, without exception or a gray area to work around it. Amazon's own review-integrity enforcement treats this category of violation seriously, and the consequences run from listing removal to account-level action. Vine exists specifically as the sanctioned channel for exactly this need; using it, rather than looking for an adjacent workaround, is the entire answer for a brand that needs early review volume without stepping outside Amazon's terms.

Which one you should actually pick

If you're a brand needing early review credibility on a new listing, Vine is the entire legitimate answer — priced by unit count, billed only once it's actually worked, and capped at 30 reviews per product. There's no compliant alternative outside it that pays or trades for a review, and no honest version of this answer suggests otherwise.

Common questions

How does a seller get reviewers for a new Amazon product?

The only Amazon-sanctioned path for early, review-focused seeding is the Vine program — enrolling a product for Amazon's own trusted Vine Voice reviewers to request and review. Sellers cannot choose, contact or compensate individual reviewers directly outside this program without breaching Amazon's terms.

How much does Amazon Vine cost?

$0 for up to 2 enrolled units, $75 for 3–10 units, and $200 for 11–30 units, per parent product. The fee is only charged 30 days after enrollment and after the first review publishes — if no review publishes within 90 days, the fee is waived.

Does Amazon Vine guarantee positive reviews?

No. Vine Voices write genuine, honest reviews, which can be critical if the product doesn't hold up. Vine supplies review volume and credibility, not a guaranteed outcome on sentiment — the product's actual quality still determines what the reviews say.

Can I offer a discount or free product for a review outside of Vine?

No — incentivizing a review outside the Vine program breaches Amazon's terms, regardless of how it's structured or framed. Vine is the sanctioned channel for exactly this need; there's no compliant workaround outside it.

Is Amazon Vine only for brand-new products?

Effectively yes — enrollment requires the product to have fewer than 30 reviews at the time of enrollment, which makes it a launch tool rather than something usable to boost an already-established listing's review count.
