What Amazon FBA Actually Is

Fulfillment by Amazon means you ship inventory to Amazon's warehouses, and Amazon handles picking, packing, shipping, returns, and customer service from there. You still own the listing, the pricing, and the marketing. Amazon owns the box on the shelf and the truck it leaves on.

Amazon runs two seller plans, and the difference matters more than beginners expect. The Individual plan charges per item sold with no monthly fee. The Professional plan charges a flat monthly subscription with no per-item fee. Most beginners start on Individual to test the water, then move to Professional within a few months once volume makes the flat fee cheaper than the per-item charge. Check Seller Central for the current numbers on both — Amazon adjusts them periodically, and a stale figure from a blog post is worse than no figure at all.

FBA is not the only fulfillment model. Fulfilled by Merchant (FBM) means you ship orders yourself. FBA trades control and margin for speed and the Prime badge. Neither is automatically better — it depends on your product's size, velocity, and margin.

The Four Fee Buckets You're Actually Paying

Every FBA seller pays into four separate buckets, and beginners usually only budget for one of them (the fulfillment fee) and get surprised by the other three. None of these are optional add-ons — they're structural, and they apply whether or not you ever run an ad.

  • Referral fee: Amazon's cut of each sale, a percentage that varies by category.
  • Fulfillment fee: covers pick, pack, ship, customer service, and returns handling for that unit — priced by size and weight tier, not by price point.
  • Monthly storage fee: charged on the space your inventory occupies in a fulfillment center, and it moves with the season — it's higher in the run-up to Q4.
  • Aged inventory surcharge: stock that sits too long (Amazon's threshold is currently around six months) gets charged extra, on top of standard storage.

A Worked Example: Where the Money Actually Goes

Say you're selling a kitchen gadget at $24.99, and it costs you $7.50 landed (product plus inbound freight). For the sake of the example, assume a 15% referral fee for the kitchen category, a fulfillment fee of $5.20 for a small standard-size item, and $0.35 in monthly storage. That's roughly $3.75 + $5.20 + $0.35 = $9.30 in Amazon fees, against $7.50 in product cost — total cost of $16.80, leaving about $8.19 before you spend a cent on advertising.

That last part is the beginner trap. Advertising is not optional on Amazon; it's how you get found against everyone else selling something similar. If your unrealistic-margin product needs $6 of ad spend per sale to convert, you've gone from an $8.19 margin to just over $2 — and that's before returns, which FBA also bills you for.

Run this math with your own numbers before you list, using the actual calculator, not a guess. If the number comes back negative, that is real information, not a rounding error to argue with.

The Mistakes Beginners Make (And a Few We've Made Ourselves)

The single biggest mistake is pricing a product before knowing its true landed fee stack. Sellers pick a price that looks competitive, then discover the fulfillment fee tier is higher than expected because the packaging pushed the item into a bigger size bracket. Measure the box before you set the price, not after.

The second is treating FBA as a fix for a weak product. FBA solves logistics. It does not solve a product nobody wants, a listing nobody can find, or a review base that hasn't been built yet. Prime shipping gets a customer to the buy button faster — it doesn't get them there in the first place.

The third is ignoring aged inventory until the surcharge shows up. New sellers order too much stock to hit a supplier's minimum, it sells slower than projected, and six months later Amazon is charging extra for the shelf space every month it sits.

We've watched brands make a version of this mistake past the beginner stage too, at real scale: nobody rebuilds the unit economics after the business outgrows the spreadsheet it started on. Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon spend across 100+ brands, and the accounts that stall almost always have the same root cause — the fee stack and the ad budget were built for last year's size tier, not this year's.

When the Math Says No

If you run the numbers and the margin is negative or too thin to survive a single price war, don't list it hoping volume fixes it. Volume on a losing unit economy just loses money faster. There are three real fixes, in order of how much control you have over them: lower the landed cost (renegotiate sourcing, change packaging to drop a size tier), find a different product where the category referral fee and fulfillment tier actually leave room, or accept that this specific SKU isn't the one to start with.

The same applies once you're live. If your margin was fine at launch and disappears three months later, check the fee tier first — Amazon does recategorize items, and a size or weight change can push you into a different fulfillment bracket without an obvious notification. Don't assume the drop in profit means demand fell before you've ruled out a fee change.

Common questions

How much money do I actually need to start Amazon FBA?

It depends entirely on your sourcing model. Retail or online arbitrage can start small since you're buying single units at a time. Private label products need enough capital to cover a minimum order quantity from a supplier plus inbound freight, which is a bigger number and varies by product. Whatever the figure, build in a buffer for the fee stack — most beginners budget for product cost and forget storage and returns.

Is FBA still worth it for a beginner, or is FBM easier?

FBA is easier operationally — you're not packing boxes yourself — but it costs more per unit than FBM in most cases, because you're paying for that convenience and for Prime eligibility. If your product is heavy, slow-moving, or already sells well without Prime, FBM can be the better starting point. If speed and Prime badge matter for your category, FBA usually wins.

What's the most common reason a beginner's FBA math doesn't work?

Sizing. A product that looks small on a shelf can land in a bigger fulfillment fee tier once it's boxed for shipping, and that single bracket change can erase the margin a seller planned around. Measure the actual shipped dimensions before setting a retail price, not the product dimensions alone.

Do I need an LLC or business license to sell on Amazon FBA?

Amazon doesn't require a formal business entity to register a seller account, but most sellers set one up anyway for liability and tax reasons once they're selling regularly. That's a conversation for an accountant or attorney familiar with your state, not something to guess at from a blog post.

When does it make sense to bring in an agency instead of doing this myself?

Usually once the problem stops being 'how do I list a product' and becomes 'why is this account's advertising, catalogue, and supply chain not talking to each other at scale.' Most true beginners don't need that yet — the calculator and a first small batch teach more than any agency call will at that stage.
