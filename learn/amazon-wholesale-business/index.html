What 'Amazon Wholesale Business' Actually Means

There are two things people mean when they type this phrase, and they're not the same business. The first is the reseller model: you buy an established brand's products in bulk, direct from the manufacturer or an authorized distributor, and resell them on Amazon at a markup. You don't own the listing. You don't own the brand. You're one of several sellers competing on the same product page.

The second is Amazon's own Bulk & Wholesale Buying tool inside Amazon Business — a procurement feature for companies buying office supplies, IT gear, or breakroom stock at volume discounts. That's a purchasing tool for buyers, not a business model for sellers. If you searched this phrase looking to start a business, the second thing isn't it. This page is about the first.

Wholesale is different from private label, where you create your own product and own listing, and different from dropshipping, where you never hold inventory. Wholesale sits in between: real inventory, someone else's brand, shared control of the price and the Buy Box.

The Math That Decides Whether It Works

Say you buy a case pack of 144 units at $6 a unit from a distributor — $864 landed. The item sells on Amazon for $12.99. Amazon's referral fee (roughly 15% in most categories) takes about $1.95. FBA fulfillment for a small item runs somewhere around $4.50. That leaves roughly $0.54 a unit before you've paid for storage, returns, or a single ad click. These are illustrative figures, not a quote from any supplier — the point is the shape of the math, not the exact cents.

That's the number most people don't run before they buy the pallet. Wholesale margins look fine on the wholesale invoice and get thin fast once Amazon's fee stack and the other sellers on that same listing are in the picture.

  • Referral fee: a percentage of the sale price, category-dependent.
  • Fulfillment fee: per unit, driven by size and weight, not by your cost.
  • Storage and returns: ongoing, invisible until the invoice arrives.
  • Price competition: other authorized sellers on the same listing can undercut you at any time, and often will.

Where the Margin Actually Gets Eaten

Fees are the predictable part. The part that surprises people is the Buy Box. On a private label listing, you set the price. On a wholesale listing, you're sharing it with everyone else who's also authorized to sell that brand, and the price tends toward whoever is willing to take the thinnest margin. That's a race you can lose without doing anything wrong.

Brand gating and MAP (minimum advertised price) policies add another layer. Some brands restrict who can sell their products on Amazon at all; others will deauthorize a seller for pricing below an agreed floor, even if the seller legally sourced the inventory. Neither of those is something advertising or better listing copy can fix — they're upstream, contractual problems.

The Mistake Almost Everyone Makes

The most common mistake is treating a margin problem as a marketing problem. A seller sees a wholesale SKU stalling, assumes it needs more ad spend or a better campaign structure, and pours money into a listing where the underlying unit economics were never going to support it — because five other sellers are fighting for the same Buy Box on the same margin.

We've made a version of this mistake ourselves: agreeing to run ads on a wholesale account before checking whether the price war on that listing had already made the SKU unprofitable. Across more than $500M in managed Amazon spend and 100+ brands, the pattern is consistent enough to name plainly — advertising can move a listing that has room to move. It can't create margin that the sourcing and pricing structure already removed.

What to Do When the Numbers Don't Work

If your worked example comes out thin or negative, don't run the campaign anyway and hope volume fixes it. Volume on a bad margin just loses money faster. Work the actual levers instead:

  • Renegotiate the case-pack price or MOQ — most distributors have room at higher volume that wasn't offered at the first quote.
  • Check for exclusive or limited distribution — fewer authorized sellers on a listing means less Buy Box pressure.
  • Recheck the fee category — some items qualify for lower referral tiers than the default assumption.
  • Know when to walk away from the SKU — not every wholesale line is salvageable, and holding onto one out of sunk-cost thinking is its own mistake.

If none of those levers move the number into positive territory, the honest answer is that the SKU isn't a wholesale opportunity on Amazon right now, regardless of how good the product is.

Which one you should actually pick

Wholesale suits someone comfortable sharing a listing and competing on price against other authorized sellers, not building a brand from scratch. Private label suits someone willing to spend more upfront to own the listing and the margin. Neither works if you skip the fee math first — and no amount of advertising fixes a margin problem that was already structural.

Common questions

Is an Amazon wholesale business still profitable?

It can be, but only after you run the actual math on referral fees, fulfillment costs, and Buy Box competition for that specific SKU. Wholesale margins on the supplier invoice often look fine and shrink significantly once Amazon's fee stack and other authorized sellers are in the picture.

Do I need the brand's permission to resell it on Amazon?

Often yes. Many brands restrict distribution and can gate their listing so only authorized sellers can list, or enforce a minimum advertised price. Sourcing inventory legally doesn't guarantee Amazon selling rights — check the brand's distribution policy before you buy stock.

What's the difference between Amazon wholesale and Amazon Business bulk buying?

Amazon Business's bulk buying tool is a procurement feature for companies purchasing supplies at volume discounts — it's for buyers, not sellers. An Amazon wholesale business is the seller-side model of buying branded goods in bulk to resell on an existing listing. They share a word but not a business.

Can advertising fix a wholesale listing with thin margins?

No. Advertising can move a listing that has margin room to work with. It can't create margin that's already been removed by fee structure, Buy Box competition, or a price war among authorized sellers of the same product.

How much capital does it take to start?

It depends entirely on the supplier's minimum order quantity and case-pack pricing, which vary by distributor and category — there's no single starting figure that holds across products. Ask for the MOQ and per-unit price at your target volume before estimating anything else.
