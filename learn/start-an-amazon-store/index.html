How to Start an Amazon Store

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1265 words · Written against what currently ranked for “start an amazon store”
The short answer

Starting an Amazon store means one of two different things: opening a seller account to sell products, or building a free branded Store page. The Store requires Brand Registry, which requires a registered trademark — so open the seller account first, then work toward the Store.

Two different things people mean by 'an Amazon store'

Half the people typing this phrase mean becoming an Amazon seller — creating an account, listing products, getting paid. The other half mean an Amazon Store — the free, multi-page branded storefront (think of it as your own mini-site inside Amazon) that you get to once you're enrolled in Brand Registry. Amazon's own sign-up pages blend both under one flow, which is why the confusion is so common.

These are sequential, not alternative. You cannot build a Store without a seller account and Brand Registry first. If you're picturing a branded page with your logo, banner, and product collections, you're three steps past where most people actually are.

The steps, in the order they actually happen

  • Choose a plan. Individual is per-item, no monthly fee. Professional is a flat monthly charge regardless of how much you sell, plus referral fees per order.
  • Register. Business details, bank account, tax information, ID verification.
  • List and decide fulfillment. Ship orders yourself (FBM) or send inventory to Amazon (FBA) and let them pick, pack, and handle returns.
  • File for Brand Registry. This needs a registered trademark, not a pending one. This step, not the selling account, is the actual bottleneck for most new brands.
  • Build the Store and turn on advertising. The Store doesn't get traffic on its own — Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Products are what send people to it.

The flat monthly fee is the easy part to plan around because it doesn't move with volume. The number that actually decides your margin is the referral fee, and it varies by category — check the current fee for your specific category before you price anything, because a few percentage points on every order adds up faster than the monthly plan cost ever will.

Reading the '$50,000 in incentives' headline correctly

Amazon's sign-up page advertises 'up to $50,000 in incentives.' That number is real, but it's not cash handed to you — it's a rebate structure: 10% back on your first $50,000 in branded sales, which caps out at $5,000, then 5% back on sales up to $1,000,000. Add a $100 shipping credit and up to $1,000 in Sponsored Products credit, and the actual first-year value for most new sellers is a few thousand dollars in credits and rebates, not fifty thousand.

This isn't a knock on Amazon's math — the incentives are genuinely useful, especially the ad credit and FBA shipping discount. It's just a headline number built from a ceiling, not a payout, and reading it as the latter is the single most common misunderstanding we see from people starting out.

Why the Store stalls before it opens

The most common holdup isn't Amazon's approval process — it's the trademark. Brand Registry needs a registered mark, and trademark registration in most countries takes months, sometimes longer if there's an office action. People start the seller account, get a few products live, then discover the Store they wanted is gated behind a legal filing they haven't started yet.

The second holdup is sequencing advertising and content. Across the $500M-plus in Amazon ad spend we've managed at Full Circle, the accounts that get the best return build the Store and the advertising plan at the same time — not the Store first, with ads as something to figure out later once traffic doesn't show up on its own.

The mistake that burns the ad credit fast

Amazon gives new Professional sellers up to $1,000 in Sponsored Products credit. The common mistake — and one we've made ourselves, pushing spend live to hit a launch date — is turning ads on before the listing is actually ready: no A+ content, thin bullet points, one or two photos. The credit gets spent driving clicks to a page that converts at half the rate it could, and the seller concludes 'ads don't work' when the real problem was the listing underneath them.

If your early results look bad, check the listing before you touch the bids. A low conversion rate on a new listing is almost always a content problem, not a targeting problem.

Which one you should actually pick

An Individual account suits someone testing a few products with no urgency. A Professional account plus FBA suits a brand ready to scale fulfillment and run ads. Brand Registry and a Store suit a business that already has, or is actively filing, a trademark and wants to invest in content and advertising. Full Circle works with brands past the first step — building the account, the content, and the ad plan together rather than in sequence — which is where the setup above tends to either pay off or stall.

Common questions

Do I need a registered business to start selling on Amazon?

No. You can register as an individual seller with your own name, bank account, and tax information. A formal business entity becomes more useful once you're building a brand, filing a trademark, or wanting the legal separation, but it's not required to open a seller account.

What's the real difference between Individual and Professional selling plans?

Individual charges a per-item fee and suits low, occasional volume. Professional charges a flat monthly fee regardless of volume, plus referral fees, and unlocks bulk listing tools, advertising, and eligibility for programs like Brand Registry. Most people planning to build a real store need Professional.

How long does Brand Registry actually take?

The Amazon approval step is usually fast once you have a qualifying trademark. The slow part is getting that trademark registered in the first place, which can take months and varies by country and whether there are objections. Plan the trademark filing before you plan the Store launch date.

Can I start an Amazon store without holding inventory?

You can sell without holding inventory yourself by using FBA, where you ship stock to Amazon's warehouses and they handle fulfillment. You still need to own or source the inventory at some point — Amazon doesn't manufacture or drop-ship on your behalf.

How much money do I actually need to start?

The account itself is cheap — a flat monthly fee for Professional selling, referral fees per sale, and FBA fees if you use them. The real cost is inventory and, if you want visibility, advertising spend on top of that. Budget for product cost before you budget for the platform.
