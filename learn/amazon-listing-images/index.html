Amazon Listing Images: What Every Slot Is For

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1405 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon listing images”
The short answer

An Amazon listing carries three separate image systems: the standard gallery (up to nine photos, main image on pure white), A+ Content modules (design slots once Brand Registered), and video. Each has different rules — treating them as one problem is why sellers miss requirements that apply to only one of the three.

Three systems, one page

Sellers researching "listing images" are usually looking for one answer and Amazon actually has three separate systems stacked on the same product page. The standard image gallery — up to nine slots, seven visible by default on desktop — is mandatory, category-general, and governed by Amazon's core image policy: real photography, white background on the main image, product filling most of the frame. A+ Content sits below that, is entirely optional, requires Brand Registry, and follows a completely different set of module-based rules. Video is a third system again, with its own upload path and, until recently, its own Brand Registry gate that's loosened for some sellers. Getting one right doesn't get the others right — each has separate specs, separate approval logic, and separate reasons a listing can look wrong.

It's worth naming which one a specific problem belongs to before troubleshooting it, because the fix and the help documentation are different for each. A suppressed listing is almost always a standard-gallery problem. A rejected module is an A+ Content problem. A video that won't upload is neither of those, and searching the wrong system's help pages wastes real time.

The standard gallery: what's actually required

The main image is the strictest: a pure white background (Amazon states RGB 255, 255, 255 specifically, not just "light"), real product photography rather than a render, and the product filling 85% or more of the frame — no props, no text, no badges. Secondary images have more latitude — lifestyle shots, infographics, size charts, packaging contents — though they still can't include Amazon-owned elements like the star-rating graphic or a "Best Seller" badge design. Amazon recommends at least six images and a video, covering six distinct shot types: individual, group, lifestyle, scale, detail and packaging. Every image needs to sit between 500 and 10,000 pixels on its longest side, with Amazon's own guidance preferring anything above 1,000 pixels so the zoom feature works — and every image needs to be clear, unpixelated, and free of jagged edges, a bar that's easy to fail on a phone photo shrunk and stretched to fit rather than shot or exported at the right resolution from the start.

A+ Content: a different rulebook, once you're eligible

A+ Content doesn't touch the standard gallery at all — it replaces the plain-text description below it with design modules: full-width banners, comparison charts, a brand story carousel. It needs Brand Registry to unlock at all, and its image requirements are set per-module rather than as one universal spec — a full-width banner and a three-image comparison row are different shapes with different pixel requirements, checked live inside the module builder rather than against one fixed chart. Amazon states A+ Content can lift sales up to 8% on the Basic tier and up to 20% on Premium, though that's Amazon's category figure, not a promise for any specific listing.

Video: the one that changed access rules recently

Video was historically gated behind Brand Registry the same way A+ Content is, but that's loosened — Professional sellers can now generally upload a product video directly to their own listing without Brand Registry, and separately, any customer with a buyer account (including a seller using a second account) can upload to the Related Video Shorts section that appears on most product pages. Those are two different upload paths with two different sets of rules, and confusing them is common — a seller who can't find the direct upload option because their account type doesn't have it yet often can still get video onto the page through the buyer-facing shorts path.

How this maps to what shows up on mobile

The slot count and specs above describe the desktop page. Mobile compresses the gallery into a swipeable carousel that opens on the main image and lets a shopper flick through the rest — fewer images are visible in the first glance than on desktop, and the main image is doing proportionally more work on the platform where most Amazon browsing actually happens. That's a reason to front-load the strongest secondary images (the shot that answers the most common question, not just the prettiest lifestyle photo) into the first three or four positions rather than treating the order as an afterthought once the main image is locked in.

The mistake that spans all three systems

The recurring failure isn't getting any single image wrong — it's treating the three systems as one project with one deadline. A seller who nails the standard gallery at launch, then builds A+ Content eight months later once Brand Registry finally comes through, then adds video whenever someone remembers to, ends up with three visibly different photography styles stitched onto one product page — different lighting, different backgrounds, different brand voice in the overlay text. None of the individual pieces violate a rule. Together they read as unpolished, and that's a design-management failure, not a compliance one.

Keeping all three in sync at scale

On a single listing, keeping three image systems visually consistent is a checklist. Across a catalogue where new SKUs launch monthly and Brand Registry eligibility, A+ Content access and video access don't all arrive on the same day, it becomes a scheduling problem — which is the layer Full Circle manages for the brands in our book. Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon spend across 100+ brands, and the recurring fix on a newly onboarded account is less often a broken rule and more often three photography eras visible on the same page because nobody owned the whole picture at once.

Which one you should actually pick

Treat the gallery, A+ Content and video as one creative project with three delivery dates, not three separate to-dos. A brand launching fresh should brief all three at once even if Brand Registry or video access lags a few months behind the initial launch — that keeps the visual style consistent instead of assembling it in visibly different eras across a listing's first year on the platform.

Common questions

How many images can an Amazon listing have?

Most listings support up to nine standard images, though typically only the first several display by default on desktop before a shopper clicks to see more. Amazon recommends at least six images plus a video as a practical minimum, separate from any A+ Content modules.

Do all Amazon listing images need a white background?

No — that rule applies specifically to the main image. Secondary images can use lifestyle settings, infographics and colored backgrounds, as long as they don't include Amazon-owned graphic elements like star ratings or badge designs.

Is A+ Content part of the standard image count?

No. A+ Content is a completely separate system that replaces the plain-text description, not an extension of the nine-image gallery. It has its own module-by-module image requirements and needs Brand Registry to access at all.

Why do my listing images look inconsistent even though each one passed review?

Because passing Amazon's compliance check and looking cohesive are different bars. Images added at different points — gallery at launch, A+ Content months later, video whenever it got prioritized — often use different lighting, backgrounds or brand styling even when every individual image is technically compliant.

What image resolution does Amazon actually require?

Amazon's own guidance specifies images between 500 and 10,000 pixels on the longest side, with a preference for anything above 1,000 pixels because that's the threshold the zoom feature needs to function. Below that, the image displays fine but shoppers lose the ability to zoom in on detail.
