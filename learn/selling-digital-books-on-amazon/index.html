What KDP Actually Is and What It Costs

Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP) is the only way to sell a digital book directly on Amazon. It's free to join — there's no listing fee, no monthly subscription, and no fee to upload a manuscript. You keep control over price, content, and territory rights.

You publish in one of three formats: eBook, paperback, or hardcover, and you can list all three against the same book page. Amazon builds the sales page, handles the transaction, delivers the file to the reader's device, and pays you monthly.

The catch isn't a platform fee — there isn't one in the traditional sense. The catch is that Amazon's royalty math has more moving parts than the sign-up page lets on, and most of what goes wrong happens inside those parts.

The Royalty Math: A Worked Example

Say you price an eBook at $4.99. If it falls inside the 70% royalty band — list price between $2.99 and $9.99 — you earn 70% of that price, minus a delivery fee based on the file's size in megabytes. A 3MB file, roughly a novel with a simple cover, costs about $0.15 to deliver.

So: $4.99 × 0.70 = $3.49. Subtract the $0.15 delivery fee and you're paid $3.34 per copy. Price the same book at $1.99 or $11.99 — outside the $2.99–$9.99 band — and you drop to the 35% tier with no delivery fee deducted: $0.70 or $4.20 per copy. On an $11.99 book, the 35% tier actually pays less per unit than pricing it at $9.99 under the 70% tier would.

Print works differently. Paperback and hardcover pay up to 60% royalty, but Amazon deducts a printing cost first, calculated from page count, trim size, and ink type, not file size. A 300-page black-and-white paperback and a 300-page full-color paperback are not the same cost to print — Amazon shows the exact figure before you set a price, but only if you check it.

KDP Select and Kindle Unlimited: The Trade-Off

KDP Select is a separate, optional enrollment that puts your eBook into Kindle Unlimited and the Kindle Owners' Lending Library. Readers borrow it for free and you get paid per page read out of a shared monthly fund that Amazon sets — not a fixed royalty. Amazon publishes the fund size after the fact: total KDP KU author earnings for July came in at $71.0 million, split across every enrolled page read that month. Your share depends on how many pages of your book were read relative to everyone else's, so the same book can earn a different amount in two consecutive months without you changing anything.

The trade you're making for that exposure is exclusivity: while enrolled, you cannot sell the eBook edition anywhere else — no Apple Books, no Kobo, no direct sales from your own site. The term is 90 days and auto-renews unless you turn it off inside the KDP dashboard. Authors who forget this find themselves locked out of another platform's promotion because a KDP Select term renewed itself in the background.

The Mistake That Costs Authors the Most

The single most common mistake is pricing a book without checking which royalty band it lands in — usually by accident, when a currency conversion or a promotional discount pushes the list price a few cents outside $2.99–$9.99 in one marketplace even though it's inside the band in another. Because KDP prices are set per marketplace, a book can be in the 70% tier in the US and the 35% tier in the UK at the same nominal price, purely from exchange-rate rounding.

The second mistake is ignoring file size on an illustrated or image-heavy book — children's books, cookbooks, comics. A 40MB file at roughly $0.15/MB in delivery fees costs $6.00 in fees before Amazon even applies the royalty percentage, which can erase most of the margin on a $6.99 picture book. Authors who format for print first and convert that same file for Kindle often ship a file three or four times larger than it needs to be, because print-resolution images carry over untouched.

The mistake we see most often, honestly, is treating the royalty percentage as the whole answer and never opening the pricing page to see the actual deduction sitting next to it. Amazon shows both numbers before you publish. Most people only read one of them.

When Your Royalty Report Looks Wrong

If a royalty report looks lower than the math above suggests, work through it in this order:

  • Check the price tier per marketplace. The same book can sit in different royalty bands in the US, UK, and EU stores at the same nominal price.
  • Check the delivery fee against file size. KDP shows this number on the pricing page — if you haven't looked at it since your last file upload, it may have changed.
  • Check whether KDP Select renewed. If a book earns Kindle Unlimited page-read income you weren't expecting, or loses eligibility to sell elsewhere, the 90-day term likely auto-renewed.
  • Check the territory royalty rate. Some countries pay a reduced rate regardless of price, independent of the 70/35 split.

None of these are bugs. They're all disclosed in the KDP dashboard, on different screens, which is exactly why a number that's technically correct still surprises people.

Which one you should actually pick

KDP direct is the right call for almost every solo author and small publisher — it's free, keeps control with you, and the royalty math is public even when it isn't simple. An aggregator only earns its cut if you need distribution beyond Amazon. Common questions

Can I sell an eBook on Amazon without going through KDP?

For direct sales in the Kindle Store, no — KDP is Amazon's only self-publishing route. Some authors go through a distribution aggregator that pushes the same file to Amazon and other retailers at once, but the aggregator takes an additional cut on top of Amazon's royalty structure, and the 70/35 tier logic still applies underneath it.

Is the 70% royalty rate real, or is it lower after fees?

The 70% is real, but it's calculated before the delivery fee is subtracted, not after. On a typical text-only novel that fee is small — a few cents. On a large, image-heavy file it can take a meaningful bite out of the payout, which is why the effective rate on two $4.99 books can differ.

Does enrolling in KDP Select hurt my sales elsewhere?

It removes the option to sell the eBook edition anywhere but Amazon for the length of the enrollment. Print editions aren't affected. The enrollment is 90 days and renews automatically unless you cancel it inside the dashboard, so it's worth calendaring the renewal date if you're weighing another platform.

Why did my royalty payment come in lower than I expected?

Start with the price tier in the specific marketplace the sale happened in, then the delivery fee shown against your current file size, then whether KDP Select silently renewed. These are the three most common causes, and none of them require a support ticket to check — they're all visible in the KDP dashboard.

Do I need an ISBN to sell a digital book on Amazon?

No. KDP assigns a free ASIN to every eBook automatically. An ISBN is optional and mainly relevant if you're publishing a paperback and want it trackable outside Amazon's own catalogue system.
