Charge From Amazon Marketplace: Buyer Statement or Seller Fee?

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1679 words · Written against what currently ranked for “charge from amazon marketplace”
The short answer

A charge from Amazon Marketplace is either a buyer-side bank statement line (like AMZN Mktp US) tied to an order, or a seller-side deduction — referral fee, fulfillment fee, storage, advertising — taken from a seller's payout. They're unrelated and they show up in completely different places.

Two Different Charges Hide Behind the Same Phrase

If you searched this because of a bank statement, you're a shopper trying to identify a line like AMZN Mktp US or AMAZON MKTPLACE PMTS. That's a real order, a digital purchase, or a Prime payment, and the fix is usually a five-minute look at your order history.

If you searched this because a payout looked smaller than expected, you're a seller, and the charge you're chasing is one of the fees Amazon takes out before the money ever reaches your bank account. That's a different problem with a different set of reports to check. This page covers both, because the search term doesn't tell us which one you are.

If You're a Shopper: Decoding the Bank Statement Line

Amazon's payment descriptors are inconsistent on purpose across banks, but the pattern is predictable. A line starting with AMZN Mktp or Amazon.com is a retail order. A line with Prime in it is your membership renewal, monthly or annual depending on your plan. A line referencing a company name you don't recognize is usually an Amazon Pay transaction — a purchase made on someone else's site using your Amazon-stored card, which is why it doesn't look like an Amazon order at all.

The most common source of confusion isn't fraud, it's a bank authorization. When you place an order, Amazon contacts your bank to confirm the card works. That authorization shows up on your statement immediately, before the item ships and sometimes even if the order is cancelled. It looks identical to a real charge but it isn't one — it drops off on its own, usually within a few business days depending on your bank.

The other common cause: one order shipped in pieces. Amazon charges each shipment separately when it leaves the warehouse, not when you check out. Three items shipping on three different days produces three separate charges, each smaller than the order total, which reads as suspicious until you match the amounts against your order number.

If You're a Seller: What Amazon Actually Deducts From Your Payout

On the seller side, "charge" means something else entirely: money Amazon subtracts from what a sale would otherwise pay out. These aren't hidden — they're itemized in the Payments report inside Seller Central — but they're easy to miss if you're only glancing at the deposit total.

  • Referral fee — Amazon's percentage of the sale price, set by category, taken on every completed order.
  • FBA fulfillment fee — pick, pack, and ship, charged when Amazon handles the inventory, priced by size tier and weight rather than by item price.
  • Storage fee — monthly rent on whatever is sitting in an Amazon warehouse, billed by cubic foot and higher in Q4.
  • Refund administration fee — Amazon keeps a portion of the referral fee even after a customer return is processed.
  • Advertising charge — Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, or Sponsored Display spend, billed separately from the order itself against your ad payment method or invoicing threshold.

Advertising is the one we deal with directly, and across the $500M+ in Amazon ad spend we've managed for more than 100 brands, it's consistently the line item that looks wrong before anyone's actually checked why. Spend that clears faster than a payout report updates, or a bid strategy that front-loads a budget, reads exactly like an error until you pull the invoicing history.

A Worked Example: Where the Money Actually Goes

Take a $30 item in a category sitting at Amazon's common 15% referral fee. Amazon takes $4.50 off the top before anything else happens. If Amazon is fulfilling the order, add a fulfillment fee based on size and weight, not price. Add storage rent for however long the unit sat in the warehouse. Add any advertising spend that was attributed to that SKU. What lands in the seller's bank account is well under $30, and none of the individual numbers are fixed — referral rates vary by category, fulfillment fees vary by size tier, and both change on Amazon's schedule, not the seller's.

That gap between the sale price and the deposit is normal. It only becomes a problem worth investigating when the gap is bigger than usual, or when a fee that used to apply suddenly doesn't, or the other way round.

When the Charge Still Doesn't Add Up

For shoppers: if a line doesn't match anything in Your Orders or Your Transactions after checking authorizations and split shipments, the next step is your bank, not Amazon's help pages — a genuinely unrecognized charge on a card is a bank fraud case, not a marketplace dispute.

For sellers: reconcile against the Payments report by transaction, not by total. If a specific fee jumped, check Seller Central's fee schedule change notices before assuming it's an error — Amazon updates referral fee categories and FBA size tiers on a set calendar, and sellers who don't track the calendar experience every scheduled change as a surprise. If advertising spend is the line in question, pull the Advertising Console's billing history against the campaign's own reporting; the two should match to the day.

The Mistake Most People Make With These Charges

Shoppers assume every unfamiliar line is fraud and cancel a card before checking Your Transactions. Sellers assume a smaller payout means Amazon made an error, when it's more often a fee bracket that shifted, a storage surcharge that started, or ad spend that cleared on a different day than expected.

We've made this exact mistake ourselves: chasing what looked like a billing discrepancy through Seller Support before realizing Amazon had simply recategorized the SKU into a different referral fee bracket. The fee wasn't wrong. Nobody had read the category change notice. It happens to agencies managing this daily, which is a fair warning for anyone doing it part-time.

Which one you should actually pick

Shoppers should start with Your Transactions and their bank, in that order — most "unknown" charges resolve in under five minutes. Sellers should reconcile the Payments report by line item, not by deposit total, and check Amazon's fee schedule notices before assuming an error. Both groups are usually chasing a normal process, not a mistake.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Why does my bank statement show 'AMZN Mktp US' when I don't remember ordering anything?

Check Your Transactions first — it's almost always a real order shipped in a separate piece, a family member's purchase on a shared card, a Subscribe & Save delivery, or a gift order you weren't the one who placed. If nothing on the list matches the amount or date, treat it as a bank matter, not an Amazon one.

Is a pending Amazon charge already money out of my account?

No. A pending charge is usually a bank authorization — Amazon confirming the card works before the order ships. It reserves the amount on your statement but isn't a settled charge. It clears on its own, and it can appear even for orders you later cancel.

As a seller, why did my Amazon payout drop this week?

Pull the Payments report by transaction rather than looking at the deposit total. The usual causes are a referral fee category change, a new storage surcharge, a refund administration fee from returns, or advertising spend clearing on a different schedule than usual. Amazon publishes fee schedule changes in advance — most "drops" were announced weeks earlier.

What's the difference between a referral fee and a closing fee?

The referral fee is Amazon's percentage cut on nearly every category, taken on each sale. A closing fee is an additional flat charge that applies only to specific categories, mainly media items like books and DVDs. Most sellers outside media categories will never see a closing fee at all.

Who do I contact to dispute a charge — my bank or Amazon?

If you're a shopper and the charge matches an order in Your Transactions, resolve it through Amazon customer service. If nothing matches and the charge is genuinely unrecognized, it's a bank fraud dispute. If you're a seller disputing a fee, that goes through Seller Support with the specific transaction ID, not through your bank at all.
