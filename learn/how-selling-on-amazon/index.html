The short answer

Selling on Amazon means registering a seller account, picking Individual or Professional plan, listing products, choosing fulfillment (FBA or you ship it yourself), and paying Amazon a referral fee plus fulfillment costs on every sale. What's left after that, minus your product cost, is your actual margin.

What 'selling on Amazon' actually means

It means you list your own products in Amazon's marketplace and Amazon takes a cut of every sale, either as the buyer's checkout page or, if you use FBA, as your warehouse and shipping department too. This is different from Amazon buying your inventory outright (Vendor Central) — most small and mid-size brands sell as a third-party seller, which is what this page covers.

There are two account types. An Individual plan has no monthly fee but charges a per-item fee on every sale and blocks you from advertising, bulk tools, and most growth programs. A Professional plan runs a flat $39.99/month (Amazon's own published rate for that tier) and unlocks Sponsored Products, Brand Registry eligibility, and the reporting you need to actually run the business rather than just list on it. Almost anyone planning to sell more than a handful of items a month should be on Professional from day one.

The steps, in order

  • Choose your plan. Individual or Professional — see above.
  • Register. You'll need business or personal ID, a bank account for payouts, and tax information. Verification can take anywhere from a day to a couple of weeks depending on how clean your documents are.
  • Decide fulfillment. FBA means Amazon stores, picks, packs, and ships your inventory and handles most customer service; you ship pallets or boxes to their warehouses and they do the rest. FBM (Fulfilled by Merchant) means you ship every order yourself. FBA gets you the Prime badge and generally converts better; FBM keeps more control and can be cheaper for slow-moving or oversized items.
  • Build the listing. Title, images, bullet points, backend search terms, and a category. This is also where most listings quietly lose money — a weak listing makes every dollar of advertising less efficient, and no fee structure fixes that.
  • Enroll in Brand Registry if you own the brand. It's free if you have a registered trademark, and it's the single biggest lever against counterfeit sellers hijacking your listing.
  • Go live, then watch the data before you spend on ads. Sales velocity, return rate, and Buy Box status tell you whether the listing can support paid traffic yet.

What a sale actually nets you: a worked example

Say your product sells for $30. Subtract Amazon's referral fee — a percentage of that price set by category, and one you should look up in Seller Central before you ever settle on a retail price, because it varies a lot between, say, apparel and electronics. If you're on FBA, subtract a per-unit fulfillment fee based on the item's size and weight, plus a monthly storage charge for the space your inventory sits in. Divide the $39.99/month Professional plan fee across however many units you move that month and take that off too. What's left is what you actually keep, before your own product cost and any advertising spend.

Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon spend across 100+ brands, and this pattern holds across nearly all of them: sellers who price off the list price alone, without running this subtraction first, consistently discover their real margin is thinner than the spreadsheet they built before launch.

The mistakes that actually sink new sellers

The most common one is pricing a product before checking the referral fee and fulfillment cost for that specific category — not an approximate one. The second is launching a full catalog on day one instead of proving the unit economics on one SKU first; we've told clients to do exactly that and had to walk it back more than once, so it's not a mistake unique to first-time sellers.

Other recurring ones: skipping Brand Registry and then discovering a counterfeit seller on your listing with no fast way to remove them; not accounting for the higher storage fees that apply in the run-up to Q4; and treating advertising as a fix for a listing that isn't converting organically. Advertising amplifies whatever the listing already does — it doesn't repair it.

When the numbers don't work out

If your margin comes out negative once you've subtracted referral and fulfillment fees honestly, don't lower the price to compete — that makes the problem worse, not better. Check whether a smaller package size moves you into a cheaper FBA size tier, whether the category referral fee is actually lower for a more accurately chosen subcategory, or whether the product simply needs a higher retail price than you planned.

If a listing you've had live for months is suddenly losing the Buy Box or converting worse, check for a new competitor at a lower price, a change in your own inventory or lead time, or a content edit that got reverted — Amazon's systems occasionally roll back listing content, and it looks identical to a self-inflicted mistake until you check the change history.

Common questions

How much does it cost to start selling on Amazon?

The Professional plan is a published $39.99/month. On top of that, every sale carries a referral fee (a percentage set by category) and, if you use FBA, a per-unit fulfillment fee plus monthly storage. The Individual plan skips the monthly fee but charges per item and blocks most growth tools, so it rarely makes sense past a handful of listings.

How long does it take to start selling on Amazon?

Account registration and verification can take anywhere from a day to a couple of weeks depending on how clean your business documents and bank details are. Building a real listing — photos, copy, backend keywords — usually takes longer than the registration itself if you're doing it properly.

Is FBA worth it compared to shipping orders myself?

FBA generally converts better because it comes with the Prime badge and Amazon handles customer service and returns, but you pay for that in fulfillment and storage fees. For slow-moving, bulky, or low-margin items, shipping it yourself (FBM) can keep more money on the table. Most brands end up running a mix once they have real sales data to compare.

Do I need a trademark or LLC to sell on Amazon?

You don't need either to register as a seller — an individual can sell under their own name and tax ID. You do need a registered trademark to enroll in Brand Registry, which is the main protection against other sellers hijacking your listing, so it's worth pursuing early if you're building a brand rather than reselling.

What's the difference between selling on Amazon and Amazon Vendor Central?

As a third-party seller, you set your own prices, own your inventory until it sells, and keep the relationship with the customer. Under Vendor Central, Amazon buys your inventory wholesale and resells it, sets its own retail price, and you lose most of that control. Vendor Central is invite-only and suits a smaller set of larger, established suppliers.
