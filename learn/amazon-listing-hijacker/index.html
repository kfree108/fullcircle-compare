What a hijacker actually is

A hijacker is a seller who adds an offer to your existing listing — the same ASIN, the same buy box — without authorization, usually selling counterfeit product, gray-market inventory sourced outside your authorized channel, or in some cases genuine product bought elsewhere and resold in a way that violates your distribution terms. Because Amazon groups all sellers of a given ASIN onto one listing, the hijacker's offer competes for the same buy box as yours, and buyers who land on your listing and buy from the hijacker have no way of knowing their order isn't coming from you — which is where the real damage happens, in returns, complaints and reviews attributed to your brand for a product you never shipped.

This is structurally different from a standard buy-box competition between two authorized sellers of the same genuine product, which is a normal and permitted part of how Amazon works. The distinguishing question is authorization and product authenticity, not simply whether another seller appears on your listing at all.

The path that actually works: Brand Registry

If your brand is enrolled in Amazon Brand Registry, the Report a Violation tool inside the Brand Registry dashboard is the direct, built-for-this-purpose path. You submit the ASIN, evidence of your trademark, and evidence the reported seller's offer is unauthorized or counterfeit — a comparison of the product listed to your actual product is often the most persuasive piece of evidence. Amazon investigates and, when the report is upheld, removes the offending seller's offer. This is meaningfully faster and more reliable than general seller support, because it's a dedicated queue built specifically for brand-owner IP enforcement rather than a general complaint routed through the same system handling every other kind of seller issue.

Without Brand Registry: what's genuinely available

Brand Registry requires a registered trademark — a pending trademark application can get you partial access, but full hijacker-removal capability specifically needs a registered mark. Without it, the honest picture is that direct options are limited: a general seller support case citing counterfeit or unauthorized-seller concerns can sometimes get traction, particularly with strong evidence, but it doesn't have the dedicated review queue or speed that Report a Violation does. If a trademark application is in progress, escalating to registration is the single highest-leverage move available, because it unlocks the tool built specifically for this problem rather than routing the same evidence through a slower, more general channel.

A worked example: what a strong report actually contains

A brand notices a third-party seller has added an offer to their bestselling ASIN at a price roughly 30% below their own, and a spike in one-star reviews mentioning a product that doesn't match. A strong Report a Violation submission for this case includes: proof of trademark ownership, a side-by-side comparison showing the hijacker's actual shipped product against genuine stock (sourced from a test purchase, where practical), and the specific complaint pattern in reviews that indicates the hijacker's product differs materially from the authentic item. A vague report — "someone is selling on my listing and I don't think it's authorized" — with no supporting evidence takes longer and succeeds less often than one built around concrete, comparable proof.

What to do while a report is under review

A hijacker report isn't instant, and the listing stays live with the unauthorized offer competing for the buy box during review. In the meantime, the practical levers are pricing (competing on price where sustainable, though this shouldn't be the long-term answer) and customer communication — if reviews are coming in for a product customers didn't receive from you, responding where possible to clarify authenticity, and keeping records of the pattern for the report itself. Pausing advertising spend on the ASIN isn't usually necessary unless the hijacker has taken the buy box outright, in which case ad clicks may be landing on their offer rather than yours — worth checking before continuing to spend.

The mistake that makes a weak case weaker

The most common mistake in a first-time hijacker report is submitting it too early with too little evidence, hoping speed matters more than strength — a thin report gets a thin response, and a rejected or inconclusive first report can make a follow-up report on the same seller look repetitive rather than escalating, even when the second submission has stronger evidence. It's usually worth spending an extra day gathering a genuine product comparison or documenting the review pattern properly rather than filing immediately with whatever's on hand. A well-built first report is more likely to resolve the issue outright than a fast, thin one followed by a stronger second attempt.

Why this is worth building a standing process around

One hijacker incident is a report to file. A catalogue with real sales volume attracts hijackers repeatedly, not as a one-time event, and the brands that handle it best have a standing process — trademark and Brand Registry current, a template for what evidence a strong report needs, and someone actually monitoring buy box ownership rather than discovering a hijacker from a customer complaint. Which one you should actually pick

Brand Registry with a registered trademark is the real answer here — everything else is a slower, less reliable substitute. If you're not yet enrolled, getting there is the highest-leverage single move against a hijacker, more valuable than any individual report filed through general support in the meantime. When you do file, build the strongest case you can on the first attempt rather than the fastest one.

Common questions

How do I remove a hijacker from my Amazon listing?

If you're in Brand Registry, use the Report a Violation tool in the Brand Registry dashboard — submit trademark proof and evidence the offer is unauthorized or counterfeit. Amazon investigates and removes the offer when the report is upheld. Without Brand Registry, a general seller support case is the fallback, though it's slower and less reliable.

Do I need a registered trademark to report a hijacker?

Full access to hijacker-removal tools needs a registered trademark. A pending application can get partial Brand Registry access, but the fastest path to reliably removing a hijacker runs through a completed registration.

How long does it take to remove a hijacker through Report a Violation?

It varies by case strength and evidence quality rather than following one fixed timeline. A well-documented report with clear trademark proof and a direct product comparison tends to resolve faster than a vague complaint with limited evidence.

Can a hijacker selling genuine product still be a violation?

Yes, in some cases — reselling genuine product outside authorized distribution terms, or in a condition or bundling that misrepresents it, can still qualify as a reportable violation even though the underlying item isn't counterfeit. The evidence needed differs from a counterfeit case, so frame the report around the actual issue.

Should I file a hijacker report immediately or gather more evidence first?

Usually worth a short delay to build a genuinely strong first report — trademark proof plus a real product comparison — rather than filing thin and fast. A weak first report that gets rejected can make a stronger follow-up look repetitive rather than escalating.
