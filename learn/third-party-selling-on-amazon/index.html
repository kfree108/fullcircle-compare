What Third-Party Selling on Amazon Really Means (And What It Costs)

The short answer

Third-party selling on Amazon means an independent business — not Amazon — lists and sells its own products through Amazon's marketplace, shown as “Sold by [Seller Name].” The seller sets the price and pays Amazon fees per sale, instead of selling wholesale to Amazon as a vendor.

What third-party selling on Amazon actually means

Two different people search this phrase. One is a shopper trying to work out why a product page shows “Sold by [Seller Name]” instead of “Sold by Amazon.” The other is a business owner deciding whether to sell that way. Both questions share the same root answer.

A third-party seller (often shortened to 3P) is any independent business that lists and sells products directly on Amazon's marketplace, rather than Amazon buying that inventory wholesale and reselling it itself — that second model is called first-party, or Vendor Central. “Sold by [Company Name]” under the Buy Box means you're looking at a 3P listing. “Ships from” tells you who physically handles the box — Amazon's fulfillment network (FBA) or the seller directly.

Amazon's own seller sign-up page states that independent sellers account for more than 60% of units sold in its store. That's not a niche way to sell — it's the default for most brands on Amazon today, including nearly every brand Full Circle has worked with across $500M+ in managed ad spend.

How the fee stack works — a worked example

Selling as a third party isn't free, and it isn't one flat cut either. Amazon charges in layers, and each layer answers a different question about the sale — see the table below for the stages.

Start with the fixed cost: a Professional selling plan runs $39.99 a month, stated plainly on Amazon's own seller page, whether you sell one unit or ten thousand. Everything after that scales with volume: a referral fee taken as a percentage of each sale (set by product category, so a kitchen brand and an electronics-accessory brand pay different rates on an identically priced item), an FBA fulfillment fee per unit if Amazon ships it (driven by size and weight tier), monthly storage fees on whatever sits in an Amazon warehouse, and — for most sellers — advertising spend on top, because organic placement alone rarely carries a listing anymore.

Add those layers up for one SKU and the gap between the list price and what actually lands in the bank account can be large enough to change whether a product is worth selling. We've rebuilt this exact stack, SKU by SKU, for clients including HexClad and Ridge, because the summary number Seller Central shows is rarely the number that tells you whether a SKU is profitable once referral fees, fulfillment fees and ad spend are all counted against it.

The mistakes brands make with third-party selling

  • Pricing off the item price alone. The most common mistake, and one we've made ourselves on a new SKU launch before the fee category was confirmed — margin looks fine until referral fees, fulfillment and ad spend actually post.
  • Assuming FBA is always cheaper. It usually wins for small, light items. It often loses for bulky or slow-moving ones once storage fees compound month over month.
  • Treating 3P and 1P as interchangeable. Moving a SKU from Vendor Central to Seller Central without resetting pricing, MAP enforcement, or ad strategy carries the old assumptions into a model that doesn't match them.
  • Not checking who's “Sold by” on your own listing. Unauthorized resellers can appear on a brand's own detail page and win the Buy Box, undercutting price without the brand noticing until sales drop.

When the numbers or the listing look wrong

If the margin on a SKU doesn't match what was expected, the first place to look isn't the item page — it's the settlement report. That's where the actual referral fee percentage charged shows up, and it occasionally differs from the category the product was assumed to be filed under. If it's wrong, Amazon has a category and fee appeal path through Seller Support. It can take more than one cycle to correct, so keep flagging it rather than assuming a single email fixed it.

If a listing shows a “Sold by” name that isn't the brand or an authorized reseller, that's not a pricing problem — it's a policy one. Brand Registry gives enrolled brands a reporting path for unauthorized third-party sellers on their own listings, and it's worth using every time it appears, not only once undercutting gets bad enough to notice.

Third-party (3P) or vendor (1P) — which fits a brand better

Third-party selling isn't automatically better than a wholesale (1P/Vendor) relationship — it depends on what a brand wants to control. Third-party keeps pricing, inventory and content in the brand's hands, which is why most brands we work with — Beardbrand, BK Beauty, Epic Gardening, The Woobles and Walkize among them — sell primarily this way. A 1P/vendor relationship trades some of that control for guaranteed purchase orders and less day-to-day operational load, which suits some categories and some retail buying teams better. Neither model is a trap. They're different trade-offs, and a brand can run both at once for different products or different retailers.

Which one you should actually pick

Third-party selling suits brands that want control over price, content and inventory and are willing to manage a fee stack that scales with volume. A 1P/vendor relationship suits brands that prefer guaranteed purchase orders and less operational load, at the cost of pricing control. Most growing brands end up primarily 3P, sometimes running a 1P relationship alongside it for specific retailers.

Common questions

Is third-party selling the same as dropshipping?

No. Third-party selling just describes who the “Sold by” name is on a listing — an independent business rather than Amazon. Many 3P sellers hold their own inventory and use FBA. Dropshipping, fulfilling orders straight from a separate supplier without holding stock, is one fulfillment choice a 3P seller can make, and Amazon restricts how it can be done under its seller policies.

Can Amazon itself be the third-party seller?

No. When Amazon buys inventory and resells it, the listing shows “Sold by Amazon” — that's first-party. Third-party specifically means the sold-by name belongs to someone other than Amazon.

What's the real difference between third-party (3P) and Vendor Central (1P)?

In a 1P/Vendor relationship, Amazon buys the product at wholesale and sets the retail price. In 3P, the seller sets the price, owns the inventory risk, and pays per-sale fees to Amazon instead of selling at wholesale.

Is it safe to buy from a third-party seller?

Generally yes. Amazon's A-to-z Guarantee covers most third-party purchases, and items fulfilled by Amazon or carrying the Prime badge get Amazon customer service directly, per Amazon's own help documentation. Check “Sold by” and “Ships from” before buying if you want to know exactly who's handling the order.

How do I know how much of a sale I'll actually keep as a third-party seller?

Pull the settlement report for that SKU rather than estimating from the referral fee percentage alone. It's the only place that shows the referral fee, any FBA fees, storage fees and the period's ad spend against that SKU side by side.
