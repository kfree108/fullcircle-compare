The short answer

Being an Amazon seller means you register an account, list products, and take on every job a retailer normally has: sourcing, pricing, fulfillment, customer service, compliance, and advertising. Amazon supplies the store and the traffic. You carry the cost stack, the inventory risk, and the operational work underneath it.

What Being an Amazon Seller Actually Means

You register a seller account, choose a plan, list products, and from that point on you are running a retail business inside Amazon's infrastructure. Amazon provides the storefront, the payment system, the search traffic, and — if you use FBA — the warehouse and delivery network. Everything else is yours: what you sell, what you charge, whether it's in stock, whether the listing converts, and whether you're still profitable after every fee is deducted.

There are two account types. An Individual plan has no monthly subscription but charges a per-item fee on every sale. A Professional plan costs $39.99 a month plus selling fees, and it's the one almost anyone running an actual brand ends up on, because it unlocks bulk listing tools, advertising, and Brand Registry eligibility. The plan fee is the smallest number in the whole equation — most new sellers fixate on it and miss the four or five costs that actually determine whether the business works.

The Real Cost Stack, Worked Through

Take a $20 item sold through FBA. Before you see a dollar of margin, Amazon takes a referral fee (a percentage of the sale price, set per category — it can run from single digits to over 20%, so check the exact rate for your category in Seller Central rather than trusting a number you read somewhere else), an FBA fulfillment fee (a flat charge per unit based on size and weight), and a monthly storage fee (charged per cubic foot, and it spikes hard in the fourth quarter). Layer in your $39.99 Professional plan, your cost of goods, and whatever you spend on advertising to get found, and the number that matters is never the sale price — it's what's left after five separate deductions.

This is the single most common blind spot for new sellers: pricing against a competitor's listing instead of against your own true cost stack. A product can look profitable at $20 and lose money the moment Q4 storage rates kick in or a category's referral fee turns out to be higher than assumed.

The Work Nobody Puts On The Signup Page

Listing a product is the easy part. Keeping it sellable is the job. That means catalogue accuracy (titles, images, and backend attributes that match what Amazon and shoppers actually search for), inventory planning (understaffing FBA slots kills your ranking as fast as an actual stockout), compliance (category approvals, safety documentation, IP claims from competitors), and review and returns handling that stays inside Amazon's rules.

Advertising sits on top of all of that, and it only works if the layer underneath is solid. In managing more than $500M in Amazon spend across 100+ brands, the pattern that shows up again and again is that ad performance is a symptom, not a cause — a listing with a broken image, an out-of-stock buy box, or an unoptimized backend search term will underperform no matter how well the campaigns are structured. Sellers who treat advertising as the whole job usually find out the hard way that it's the last domino, not the first.

The Mistake Almost Every New Seller Makes

The most common one: chasing revenue instead of margin. A seller sees units moving and calls it working, without checking whether referral fees, FBA fees, storage, and ad spend have already eaten past the point of profit. Revenue growth with shrinking margin is not traction — it's a business quietly getting worse while the top-line number goes up.

The second: turning up ad spend before the listing can convert. We've seen this on managed accounts too — increasing bids on a product page that had a weak main image or a missing bullet, and paying for clicks that never had a real chance to convert. The fix isn't more spend. It's fixing the page first, then spending to find out if the fix worked.

When The Numbers Say It Isn't Working

Sometimes the honest answer is that the current setup doesn't work, and pushing harder makes it worse. A few situations worth naming plainly:

  • Negative margin after the full cost stack. Don't run more ads to "grow through it." Re-price, re-source, or accept the product isn't viable at this cost structure.
  • A suspended or deactivated listing. This is almost always a policy or documentation issue, not a sales problem — resolve the underlying compliance flag before touching advertising or pricing.
  • Chronic stockouts. If a product keeps going out of stock during ad ramps, the constraint is supply chain, not marketing. More ad spend on an unavailable item wastes budget and can hurt organic ranking.
  • Rising ACOS with flat conversion. This usually points to a listing problem or increased competition, not a bidding problem. Check the page before you touch the campaign.

Common questions

How much does it cost to become an Amazon seller?

The Professional plan is $39.99 a month plus selling fees, on Amazon's own published pricing. On top of that you carry a category-specific referral fee, FBA fulfillment and storage fees if you use FBA, and your own cost of goods and advertising spend. There is no single total figure — check Seller Central's current fee schedule for your category rather than a number quoted elsewhere.

Is being an Amazon seller worth it?

It depends on what's left after the full cost stack, not on units sold. A product can move well and still lose money once referral fees, fulfillment, storage, and ad spend are subtracted. Worth it means positive margin after all five line items, tracked over enough time to see past a launch or a promotional spike.

Should I use FBA or fulfill orders myself?

FBA buys you Prime eligibility, Amazon's fulfillment and returns handling, and it typically costs less per unit than comparable premium carrier shipping — but you pay per-unit fulfillment fees and storage, and storage rises sharply in Q4. Fulfilling yourself (FBM) avoids those fees but you carry shipping speed, customer service, and returns entirely on your own. Sellers with slow-moving or oversized inventory often do the FBA math and find FBM cheaper for those specific SKUs.

How long before an Amazon seller becomes profitable?

There's no reliable timeline, and any specific number should be treated with suspicion — it depends on category competition, how quickly the listing converts, ad efficiency, and how fast inventory turns. The honest answer is to track margin from week one rather than wait for a milestone to check.

What's the biggest mistake new Amazon sellers make?

Increasing ad spend before the listing can actually convert, and mistaking revenue growth for a healthy business without checking what's left after fees. Both mistakes look like progress on a dashboard right up until the margin runs out.
