A+ Content Images: Choosing the Right Module for What You're Actually Answering

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1407 words · Written against what currently ranked for “a+ content images”
The short answer

A+ Content's image modules aren't interchangeable — a full-width lifestyle banner, a comparison chart, and an icon row answer different shopper questions. Pick the module by the question a shopper still has at that point in the page, not by which layout looks best in the editor. Amazon's own figures put a well-built set's sales lift at up to 20% on Premium.

Five module types, five different jobs

The A+ Content library isn't one image format with different sizes — it's several distinct jobs wearing an image wrapper. A full-width lifestyle banner answers "what does this look like in use?" A comparison chart answers "which variant is right for me?" An icon row (dishwasher-safe, BPA-free, 2-year warranty) answers a fast yes/no compliance question a shopper is scanning for, not reading for. A specification table answers a technical buyer's question directly. A brand story module answers a completely different question — "do I trust this company?" — and doesn't sell the product at all. Building modules by what looks good in the template picker, rather than which of these five questions your specific listing still has unanswered at that point in the page, is the single most common reason A+ Content underperforms even when the design quality is high.

The order matters as much as the content

A+ Content renders in the order you build it, and shopper attention drops fast as they scroll — the module in position one gets seen by nearly everyone who reaches A+ Content at all; by position five or six, only shoppers who are already close to buying are still reading. That means the highest-value question — usually the comparison chart, if your listing has real variant decisions — belongs early, not as a reward for scrolling. We routinely see brand story modules placed first, which reads well internally but asks a shopper who hasn't decided to buy yet to care about company history before they've resolved whether the product fits their need.

A worked example: three modules, three jobs, one page

Take a $45 supplement with three flavour variants and a health claim it can legitimately make. Module one: a comparison chart with all three flavours, serving size and primary ingredient dose side by side — answering the variant question first, while attention is highest. Module two: an icon row — third-party tested, no artificial sweeteners, made in a certified facility — answering the trust-and-compliance scan. Module three, placed last: the brand story, for the smaller number of shoppers who've already decided on the product and are now deciding whether to buy from this brand specifically rather than a competitor's near-identical SKU. Reverse that order and the comparison chart a shopper actually needed sits behind two modules most people never reach.

The images inside each module carry their own rules

Every A+ image still has to comply with the same content restrictions as a standard product image — no pricing or promotional text baked into the graphic, no unsupported superlative claims layered over a lifestyle photo, no competitor names or logos even inside a comparison chart. It's easy to forget these apply because A+ Content feels like a design surface rather than a product listing, and that's exactly the gap where rejected submissions come from. The technical pixel dimensions for each module type vary and change slightly between templates, so verify them against Seller Central's own upload tool for the specific module you're building rather than a fixed number — the upload prompt tells you the exact requirement live.

There's also a resolution trap specific to A+ images that doesn't apply to the standard gallery: because A+ modules render smaller on mobile than the desktop preview shows, an image that looks crisp in the desktop editor can come out soft on a phone screen if it was sized to the minimum rather than a comfortable margin above it. Sourcing photography at a higher resolution than the module's stated minimum, and letting the platform scale it down, avoids that gap entirely.

How to choose without guessing: read your own return reasons first

The fastest way to pick which module type actually earns its slot isn't a design instinct, it's your own returns and question data. If "wrong size" or "not what I expected" shows up repeatedly in returns or in the customer Q&A section under the listing, that's a comparison-chart or spec-table problem sitting unanswered above the fold, and A+ Content is the cheapest fix available before touching price or ads. If the return reasons are mostly about the product underperforming a claim rather than a mismatch in expectation, no image module fixes that — that's a product or claims problem, and no amount of A+ polish substitutes for it.

The mistake that costs the most: one image doing every job

The recurring failure across managed accounts isn't a badly designed individual module — it's using one hero lifestyle image across every module slot because it's the best photo in the shoot, rather than sourcing distinct photography for each job. A lifestyle shot that's beautiful in a full-width banner is nearly useless repeated inside a comparison chart, where the job is showing differences, not mood. Amazon's own data on Premium A+ Content states up to a 20% sales lift, and that figure assumes the format is doing five different jobs, not one image stretched across five slots.

Where this fits into the wider catalogue

Getting the sequence and job-matching right on one listing is a design exercise. Doing it consistently across a hundred-SKU catalogue, where new variants launch monthly and old comparison charts go stale the moment a discontinued flavour still appears in one, is an operations problem. Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon spend across 100+ brands, and on that book a comparison chart that still lists a flavour Amazon delisted six months ago isn't a design failure so much as a maintenance gap — the kind of thing that only gets caught by someone checking the catalogue on a schedule rather than once at launch.

Which one you should actually pick

If your listing has genuine variant or comparison decisions, lead with a comparison chart and save brand storytelling for the end. If it's a single-SKU reorder item, a strong lifestyle image and a specification table probably cover everything a shopper needs — building all five or seven slots just to use the space is the more common mistake than under-building.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

What image type should go first in A+ Content?

Whichever module answers the question most shoppers still have at that point in the page — usually a comparison chart if the listing has real variant decisions, or a specification table for a technical purchase. Save the brand story module for last; it answers a trust question that only matters once the product question is already resolved.

Can I reuse the same photo across multiple A+ modules?

You can technically upload the same image into different module slots, but it wastes the format. Each module type is built to answer a different shopper question, and one lifestyle photo repeated across five slots does the job of one module, not five.

Do A+ Content images need to follow the same rules as main product images?

Yes for content restrictions — no promotional or pricing text baked into the graphic, no unsupported claims, no competitor names. Pixel dimensions differ by module type and change between templates, so check the specific requirement inside Seller Central's upload tool for the module you're building rather than relying on a fixed number.

How many image modules should a listing actually use?

Enough to answer the real open questions on that specific product and no more — often three to four well-matched modules outperform a full five- or seven-module set built to fill every available slot with content that doesn't add a new answer.

Should the brand story module ever go first?

Rarely. It's useful when the brand name itself is the reason a shopper is on the page — a well-known label a shopper searched for by name — but for most listings it's competing against a product-fit question the shopper hasn't resolved yet, and belongs after that question is answered, not before it.
