The five ways money actually moves through Amazon

People typing this into a search bar usually mean one of five different business models, tangled together as if they were one thing. Only one of them can produce real income. The rest pay small, slow commissions on someone else's sale.

  • Selling your own product through FBA or FBM — the only path where the ceiling is set by your margin and demand, not a commission rate.
  • Amazon Associates — affiliate links that pay up to 10% commission on qualifying purchases, per Amazon's own program page.
  • The Amazon Influencer Program — shoppable video and livestream, open to creators who already have an audience, paid on the same qualifying-purchase logic as Associates.
  • Kindle Direct Publishing — royalties on self-published books, ebooks and audiobooks.
  • Advertising your own listings — not an earning method at all. It's the cost that decides whether the selling path above actually earns anything.

The rest of this page treats selling as the main event and the other four as what they are: real, but minor.

Selling your own product: the only path with real upside

A Professional selling account costs $39.99 a month plus selling fees, according to Amazon's own pricing page. That's the entry cost. What happens after it is where most people's mental math falls apart.

Take a product priced at $30. Before a dollar of profit shows up, four things take a cut: your cost of goods, Amazon's referral fee (this varies by category — check the current fee schedule for yours before you build a model, don't assume a flat rate), the fulfillment fee if you use FBA, and whatever you spend on ads to get found. For illustration only: $9 in goods, roughly $4 to $5 in referral fee, $5 in FBA pick-and-pack, and $3 to $6 in ad spend leaves somewhere between $5 and $9 of actual profit on that $30 sale — not the $21 gap between price and cost of goods a lot of new sellers plan around.

We've managed more than $500M in Amazon ad spend across 100+ brands, and the single most common gap between a seller's spreadsheet and their bank account is exactly this: ad spend that isn't in the model until it's already running.

Affiliate and influencer income: real, but small and slow

Amazon Associates pays up to 10% commission on qualifying purchases, and Associates Central states plainly that commission is paid approximately 60 days after the end of the month it was earned. That lag catches new affiliates who expect a payout on the same schedule as the sale.

The Influencer Program works the same way — commission on qualifying purchases driven by shoppable video or livestream — but Amazon is explicit that it's built for creators who already have a following, not people trying to build one from a storefront. Leveling up through Rising Star, Insider and A-List unlocks more placement, not a different commission structure.

Neither program replaces a job. They monetize traffic you already have. If you don't have traffic, building it comes first, and Amazon's own pages don't tell you that part.

The mistake almost everyone makes with the math

The number that gets people excited is revenue, not profit. Amazon's 2024 Small Business report counts over 55,000 independent sellers who generated more than $1 million in sales — that's a sales figure, and the gap between it and take-home is fees plus ad spend plus returns.

The second mistake is modeling ad spend as fixed. It isn't. As a listing scales, the cost to hold rank usually rises before it flattens — a brand spending efficiently at $10K a month doesn't spend proportionally the same at $100K. We've made this mistake ourselves on accounts early in a relationship: approving a budget increase because last month's return looked strong, without checking whether that return would hold at the new spend level.

The third: treating referral fee and FBA fee as static across a catalogue. They vary by category and by size and weight tier. A model built on one SKU's fees doesn't transfer to the next SKU without checking.

When the numbers don't work

If margin is negative or ad spend is eating the whole profit, the fix isn't to sell more. It's to find which of the four costs is actually the problem before touching price.

  • Check the fee category first. Amazon assigns referral fees by product category; a miscategorized listing can be paying the wrong rate.
  • Separate ad spend by objective. Defensive spend protecting your own branded search and offensive spend chasing new customers have different acceptable costs. Blending them into one number hides which one is failing.
  • Don't raise price to fix a margin problem you haven't diagnosed. A price increase that also drops conversion can make the fee stack worse, since referral fees scale with price.
  • If a change didn't move the number, check the account before the strategy. The most common cause of a fix that didn't work is a setting that didn't actually save, not a bad idea.

Which one you should actually pick

Selling your own product is the only Amazon path with real income potential, and it's the only one that costs real money up front. Associates and the Influencer Program suit people who already have an audience and want a side income, not a business. KDP suits writers. An agency suits sellers who've outgrown doing the fee, ad and catalogue math by hand.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

How much does it actually cost to start selling on Amazon?

A Professional selling account is $39.99 a month plus selling fees, per Amazon's own pricing page — that's the base. On top of that comes the referral fee for your category, fulfillment costs if you use FBA, and whatever you spend on ads, none of which are optional if you want the listing to be found.

Can you really make money with Amazon Associates?

Yes, but treat it as income tied to traffic you already have, not a business on its own. Commission runs up to 10% depending on category, and Amazon pays it roughly 60 days after the month it was earned — plan for that lag before counting on it.

Is the Amazon Influencer Program worth it if I don't have followers yet?

Amazon's own program is built around creators who already have an audience to route through shoppable video and livestream. It monetizes existing traffic; it doesn't build the traffic for you. Build the following first, then use the program.

What's the biggest reason people misjudge whether they're making money on Amazon?

Confusing revenue with margin. The over-$1-million-in-sales milestone Amazon publicizes for 55,000-plus sellers is a revenue number. What's left after referral fees, fulfillment, ad spend and returns is a different, usually much smaller figure, and it's the only one worth basing a decision on.

Do I need an agency to sell on Amazon?

No. Most sellers run the entire thing themselves through Seller Central and Amazon's ad console. An agency becomes worth considering once the volume is large enough that a small improvement in fee efficiency or ad spend is worth more than its cost.
