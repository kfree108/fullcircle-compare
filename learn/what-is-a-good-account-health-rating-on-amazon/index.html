But 200 is the floor, not a good target — 250, sustained for six months, is what actually unlocks Amazon's own deactivation-protection program.

The three zones, and why 200 isn't actually the goal

Every new Professional seller starts at an Account Health Rating of 200. Amazon's own zones: 200 and above reads as healthy, 100 to 199 as at risk, and 99 or below makes an account eligible for deactivation or already deactivated. Technically clearing 200 means you're not at risk — but treating 200 as a comfortable target misses what the number is actually for. It's a floor with essentially no buffer, and a single bad week of returns or a shipping delay can drop a seller sitting right at 200 straight into the at-risk band, with no cushion between healthy and at-risk to absorb a rough patch.

Why 250 is the number that actually matters

Amazon's Account Health Assurance program protects eligible sellers from sudden deactivation, giving them 72 hours to resolve an issue instead of an immediate account suspension — a materially different experience if something goes wrong. Eligibility requires an Account Health Rating of 250 or higher, maintained for at least six months with no more than roughly 10 days spent below that line, a Professional selling plan, at least a year of account history, and a valid emergency contact number on file. 250, not 200, is the number worth treating as the real target, because it's the one that converts account health from a constant risk into a metric with an actual safety net behind it, one that pays off precisely on the day something goes wrong through no clear fault of the seller.

How the score actually moves

The rating reflects policy compliance and selling performance, generally over roughly the trailing 180 days, and it isn't purely a penalty score — order volume itself contributes. Sellers report the score building at a rate tied to trailing six-month order volume, meaning a smaller-volume seller with genuinely flawless performance can still sit below the 250 line simply because there isn't enough sales history yet to have accumulated the points, not because anything is actually wrong. That's worth knowing before assuming a lower score always signals a compliance problem — sometimes it's a volume-timing issue that resolves itself as sales history builds, and no amount of extra compliance effort accelerates a purely volume-driven gap.

A worked example: two sellers, same compliance record, different scores

Seller A runs a smaller catalogue doing roughly 150 orders over six months, zero policy violations, ODR comfortably under 1%. Seller B runs a larger operation doing several thousand orders in the same window with a similarly clean compliance record. Despite equally clean performance, Seller B's score sits meaningfully higher, purely because the score has an order-volume component baked in alongside compliance. Seller A isn't doing anything wrong — the score simply hasn't had the sales volume to build past a certain point yet, and that's a different situation than a seller whose score is low because of actual violations. Chasing the same 250 target on the same timeline as a much larger seller, without accounting for this, is a common source of unnecessary worry on a genuinely clean account.

What to do if your score is below 250 and you want it there

Two separate levers, and it's worth knowing which one you're actually pulling. If the score is low because of compliance issues — late shipments, order defects, policy flags — fixing the underlying operational cause is the only real lever, and it moves slowly because the rating reflects a trailing window, not yesterday's fix. If the score is low mainly due to volume, sustained sales growth over time closes the gap on its own, without a specific "account health" project needed. Diagnosing which situation you're actually in, rather than assuming the worse one, changes what's worth spending time on and what isn't.

What the score doesn't tell you on its own

A single Account Health Rating number is a summary, not a diagnosis — it can sit comfortably above 250 while one specific metric underneath it (Order Defect Rate, Late Shipment Rate, Valid Tracking Rate) is quietly trending toward its own individual threshold. Amazon's Account Health dashboard breaks these out individually below the headline score, and checking the component metrics rather than only the summary number is how a developing problem gets caught before it drags the composite score down. Watching only the single number is a common way an account misses an early warning sign that was already visible one layer down.

Does the score reset if you switch selling plans or take a break?

Switching from Individual to Professional, or the reverse, doesn't zero out the rating — it's tied to the account's compliance and performance history, not the plan type active at a given moment. A period of low or paused sales activity doesn't actively damage the score the way a policy violation does, but because the score has a volume component, an extended pause can slow how quickly it climbs back toward 250 once selling resumes, simply because the trailing order-volume window has less recent activity to draw on than it would after a period of steady, ongoing sales.

Why this is worth tracking proactively, not reactively

Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon spend across 100+ brands, and the accounts that get genuinely surprised by an account health issue are almost always the ones that only checked the dashboard after something already went wrong. Checking the score and its trend on a schedule — not just when a warning email arrives — is what actually keeps 250 within reach rather than something you notice you've fallen well below only once a warning email has already landed.

Common questions

Is 200 a good Account Health Rating?

It's technically in the healthy zone, but it's the floor, not a comfortable target — there's little buffer before a bad week drops a score sitting at 200 into the at-risk band. 250, sustained for six months, is the more meaningful target because it unlocks Amazon's own deactivation-protection program.

What is Account Health Assurance?

A program that gives eligible Professional sellers 72 hours to resolve certain issues instead of facing immediate deactivation. Eligibility requires a 250+ Account Health Rating maintained for roughly six months, at least a year of account history, and a valid emergency contact on file.

Why is my Account Health Rating low even though I have no policy violations?

The score has a sales-volume component alongside compliance — a smaller-volume seller with a completely clean record can still sit below 250 simply because there isn't yet enough order history to have built the score higher, not because of any actual issue.

How often does the Account Health Rating update?

It reflects performance and compliance over a rolling recent window, generally described as around the trailing 180 days, so it moves gradually rather than jumping immediately after a single order or a single fixed issue.

Can my Account Health Rating be high while one specific metric is trending badly?

Yes — it's a composite score, and Amazon's dashboard breaks out individual metrics like Order Defect Rate and Late Shipment Rate separately below the headline number. Checking those component metrics directly catches a developing problem earlier than watching only the summary score.
