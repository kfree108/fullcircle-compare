What Is the Amazon Buy Box? (Amazon Now Calls It the Featured Offer)

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1570 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon buy box”
The short answer

The Buy Box is the Add to Cart / Buy Now section on an Amazon product page. When several sellers list the same item, Amazon's algorithm picks one offer to feature there based on price, stock, fulfillment speed, and seller performance metrics. Amazon officially renamed it the Featured Offer in 2024.

What the Buy Box Actually Is

When you land on a product page and hit Add to Cart or Buy Now, you're buying from whichever seller Amazon has decided to feature at that moment. That featured slot is the Buy Box. If other sellers offer the same item, they're pushed down into an "Other sellers on Amazon" link that most shoppers never click.

Amazon itself has moved away from the term "Buy Box" in its own documentation, calling it the Featured Offer instead — the mechanics are identical, just a name change on the seller-facing side. Most third-party guides, and most sellers, still say Buy Box, so both terms describe the same thing. It's worth knowing the rename happened, because it explains why Seller Central screens (Pricing Health, Manage All Inventory) now say "Featured Offer eligible" rather than "Buy Box eligible."

One offer can be featured, or none can — a listing can show "See options" instead of a Featured Offer if nobody currently meets the bar. That's a different problem from losing a Buy Box you used to have, and it needs a different fix.

How Amazon Decides Who Wins It

There's no single number that wins it. Amazon runs a weighted comparison across several inputs, and no one outside Amazon has the exact formula. What's consistently observable across accounts is which factors move the needle:

  • Landed price — item price plus shipping, not just the sticker price. A $19.99 item with $4 shipping loses to $22 with free shipping.
  • Fulfillment method — FBA and Seller Fulfilled Prime start with an advantage because delivery speed and customer service are handled to Amazon's standard automatically.
  • Order Defect Rate — Amazon's own published target is under 1%. Above that, eligibility gets shaky regardless of price.
  • Stock availability — an out-of-stock listing has zero chance, immediately, no exceptions.
  • Delivery date certainty — a tighter delivery window beats a vague one, even at the same price.

Across the accounts we've managed — $500M-plus in tracked Amazon spend across 100+ brands — the pattern holds: price is necessary but rarely sufficient on its own. We've watched brands cut price repeatedly to chase the Featured Offer while their real blocker was stock consistency or a defect rate creeping toward 1%. The price cut didn't fix it, and it cost margin.

A Worked Example: Same Item, Two Sellers

Here's the mechanic in numbers, using a hypothetical to show how the weighting plays out — not a real listing, just the math sellers actually run into.

Seller A lists a $24.99 item, ships FBM, and has an on-time delivery rate that puts the estimated arrival two days later than average. Seller B lists the same item at $26.49, fulfills via FBA, and has Prime-speed delivery. On price alone, Seller A wins by $1.50. On landed price plus delivery certainty, Seller B often takes the Featured Offer, because Amazon is comparing total customer experience, not just the tag on the box.

Now change one variable: Seller A's Order Defect Rate ticks up from 0.6% to 1.3% after a run of late shipments. Even at the lower price, Seller A's offer becomes ineligible for the Featured Offer entirely — not deprioritized, removed from consideration until the rate recovers. That's the part flat "lowest price wins" advice leaves out.

Eligible But Not Winning? Check These, In Order

If you're Buy Box eligible but your share is low or falling, work through this sequence before touching price again:

  • Stock consistency, not just current stock. Repeated stockouts hurt you even after you restock — Amazon remembers the pattern.
  • Pricing Health in Seller Central. The Pricing Opportunities tab shows exactly which offers are ineligible and why, before you guess.
  • Price parity. If the same item sells for noticeably less on your own site or another marketplace, Amazon can suppress the offer — this trips up more brands running their own DTC site than they expect.
  • ODR trend, not snapshot. A single bad week can suppress eligibility even if your trailing average looks fine; Amazon weighs recent performance harder than long-term averages.
  • Suppressed pricing flags. A price that looks like an error (too low, sometimes too high relative to reference price) can get pulled from Featured Offer consideration until you confirm it.

If none of those explain it, the honest answer is: check again in a few days. Buy Box share genuinely rotates among eligible sellers rather than locking to one winner, so a short dip isn't automatically a problem to fix.

The Mistake Sellers and Agencies Both Make

The most common mistake is treating the Buy Box as a pricing problem when it's an operations problem. Cutting price to win it back, without checking stock consistency or ODR first, burns margin and usually doesn't fix anything — we've made this call wrong ourselves early in an account before pulling the inventory data and finding the real cause was a fulfillment delay, not price.

The second mistake is running PPC hard on a listing that isn't winning the Featured Offer. Ad clicks land on a page where the buy button might belong to another seller, or to no one. That's spend with no possible return, and it's invisible unless someone is actually looking at Featured Offer status alongside the ad account — which is the kind of cross-check that's easy to skip when advertising and catalogue operations sit with different teams, or different agencies.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Is the Buy Box the same as the Featured Offer?

Yes. Amazon renamed the Buy Box to "Featured Offer" in its own seller-facing documentation. The mechanics — one offer featured with Add to Cart and Buy Now, others pushed to an "Other sellers" link — haven't changed, only the label has.

Why does my listing say "See options" instead of showing a Buy Box?

That means no offer on the listing currently qualifies to be featured. Usually it's stock (nobody has inventory), price (an offer looks like a pricing error and got flagged), or every seller on the listing failing a performance threshold. Check Pricing Health first — it tells you directly which offers are ineligible and why.

What's a good Buy Box percentage?

There's no official Amazon benchmark, and the figure varies enormously by category and competition level. Treat a falling trend as the signal worth acting on, not a specific target number — a single-seller listing can sit at 100% while a heavily contested category might see strong sellers rotate at 40-60%.

Can a brand-new seller win the Buy Box?

Yes, if they meet the requirements: a Professional selling plan, ODR under 1%, competitive landed price, and in-stock inventory. Account age helps in practice because it correlates with a longer performance history, but it isn't a stated requirement on its own.

Does losing the Buy Box affect my PPC ads?

It affects whether those ads make money. Sponsored ads can still run and generate clicks on a listing without the Featured Offer, but the click lands on a page where the buy button may belong to a competitor, or show no buy button at all. That's ad spend with no plausible return until the Featured Offer issue is fixed.
