What actually happens when you 'get started'

Getting started means five decisions, not one big leap: pick a selling plan, register the account, decide how you'll fulfill orders, price the product to survive Amazon's fees, and get the listing live. Skip the pricing math and the account still works fine — the business underneath it doesn't.

  • Selling plan: Individual (pay per item, no monthly fee, workable under roughly 40 items a month) or Professional ($39.99/month flat, needed for advertising, Buy Box eligibility, and bulk tools).
  • Registration: business details, tax ID, bank account, and a card on file for fees. Takes an afternoon if the paperwork matches exactly.
  • Category approval: some categories — grocery, certain electronics, supplements — require approval before you can list. Check this before you order inventory, not after it's already at the port.
  • Fulfillment: FBA (Amazon picks, packs, ships, and handles returns) or FBM (you or a third-party logistics provider does it). FBA buys Prime eligibility and speed; FBM can keep more margin per unit if you can ship fast yourself.
  • Listing: title, images, bullet points, backend search terms. This is the one step that decides whether the previous four were worth doing.

Brand Registry sits outside this list — it's free to enroll with a registered trademark, and it unlocks A+ content, Sponsored Brands, and protection against someone editing your listing. You don't need it for a first sale, but brands building something durable tend to get it early rather than after a hijacking forces the issue.

A worked example: does your first listing actually work?

Here's the part most getting-started pages skip: whether the number works. Take a $25 item as an illustration, not a quoted fee — referral fees are set per category, so check the current schedule for yours before pricing anything. If that category carries a 15% referral fee, that's $3.75 gone before you've spent a dollar on advertising. Add FBA fulfillment fees, which vary by size and weight tier, plus inbound shipping to Amazon's warehouses, and you might be down to $12–15 of margin on that $25 sale.

Now add the Professional plan's $39.99 monthly fee and whatever advertising budget you need to be visible in a competitive search result. If the unit economics don't clear that comfortably, the account isn't broken — the product or the price is. Do this math before you register, not after inventory has already landed at a fulfillment center.

The mistake most new sellers make

The most common mistake is pricing the product first and doing the fee math second. Sellers set a price that looks competitive against the top search results, launch, and only notice the margin problem after storage fees and ad spend have eaten through ninety days of sales. We've made a version of this mistake too — running ads against a listing whose price had never been checked against Amazon's actual fee schedule, which made every sale look profitable on paper and come out break-even in the account.

The fix isn't complicated: build referral fees, fulfillment fees, and storage costs into the price before launch, not into a spreadsheet after your first invoice arrives. Amazon publishes all three; the work is pulling them before you commit to a price, not reacting to them once they've already been charged.

When the numbers come back bad

Sometimes the honest answer is that the product doesn't work at this price on Amazon. If margin after referral fee, FBA fee, and inbound shipping is under a few dollars on a low-priced item, advertising will not rescue it — you need a higher price, a bundled listing, or a different channel. If a listing shows as active but isn't buyable, check for a missing image, a flagged word in the title, or a category restriction before assuming it's an advertising problem.

If the account gets flagged during setup — a common trigger is someone else's brand name or trademark showing up in a title or backend keyword — fix the listing before appealing. Amazon reads appeals in the order submitted, and a fix-first appeal clears faster than a defensive one.

Where this gets harder — and where agencies fit

That's the point where catalog, supply chain, and listing decisions start determining whether advertising can work at all, rather than the other way around.

Common questions

How much does it actually cost to start selling on Amazon?

Registering is free. Amazon's own Professional plan runs $39.99/month plus selling fees; Individual selling drops the monthly fee for a per-item charge instead. The real cost sits in referral fees — a percentage of each sale, set per category — and FBA fees if you use fulfillment by Amazon. Check the current fee schedule for your category before pricing anything; it varies enough between categories that a flat number here would mislead you.

Individual vs Professional — which plan should I choose?

Under roughly 40 sales a month, Individual avoids the flat fee. Above that, or if you want advertising, Buy Box eligibility, and bulk tools, Professional pays for itself quickly. Most brands planning to build something long-term start on Professional from day one rather than switching later.

Do I need Brand Registry before my first sale?

No. Brand Registry requires a registered trademark and isn't a prerequisite to list a product. It matters once you want A+ content, Sponsored Brands, or protection against someone else editing your listing — enroll early if you already hold the trademark, rather than waiting for a hijacking problem to force the issue.

How long until my first sale?

There's no fixed timeline Amazon publishes, and any specific day-count you see elsewhere should be treated with suspicion. It depends on how fast registration clears, how fast inventory reaches Amazon's warehouses if you're using FBA, and whether the listing is actually optimized before advertising starts. Skipping the pricing math doesn't speed any of that up — it just moves the problem to month two.

What's the most common reason new sellers fail early?

Running out of cash on inventory before the listing has enough reviews or ad exposure to convert well — not a lack of demand for the product itself. Pricing that ignores fees is usually the root cause underneath that cash problem.
