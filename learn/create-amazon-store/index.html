Two Different Things Are Called an "Amazon Store"

Every result on the first page of Google for this term blends two separate products. A seller account is your legal ability to list and sell on Amazon — it's the account, the ID verification, the plan you pay for. A Brand Store is a branded landing page, built inside Amazon's advertising console, that only exists once you're already selling and enrolled in Brand Registry.

If you typed "create amazon store" because you want to start selling, you need the seller account. If you typed it because you already sell and want the page that shows up when someone clicks your brand name — the multi-page catalog with your logo, your photography, your story — you need a Store, and Brand Registry comes first. This page covers both, in the order you'd actually need them.

How to Create a Seller Account (the Part Everyone Actually Needs First)

You pick a selling plan, register your business, and start listing. Amazon's own page states the Professional selling plan costs $39.99 a month plus selling fees (referral fees per category, and fulfillment fees if you use FBA) — that figure is published directly on their site, so it's safe to repeat. The Individual plan doesn't carry a flat monthly fee on the same page; it's a per-item fee structure instead, so check current pricing rather than trust a number from a third-party list.

  • Choose Individual (pay per item, no monthly fee, limited tools) or Professional (monthly fee, full toolset, required for Brand Registry and Stores)
  • Register your legal business details, tax information, and a bank account for payouts
  • Add your first listings, either as a new brand or against existing product catalog
  • Decide fulfillment: ship it yourself (FBM) or use Amazon's warehouses (FBA)

None of this creates a Store. It creates the account a Store will later sit on top of.

How to Create a Brand Store (the Storefront)

Building and maintaining a Brand Store is free, but access isn't automatic. You need Brand Registry — which means an active trademark — and once you're enrolled, the Store lives inside your Amazon Ads console, not your Seller Central account. You use Amazon's self-service Store Builder, pick a template, drag in tiles for images, video, and product grids, and submit the finished pages for review before they go live at a URL like amazon.com/yourbrand.

Amazon's own published data on Store performance is worth knowing before you build, because it changes how much effort the design deserves: shoppers who visit a Store buy 53.9% more often, add products to cart at a 52.1% higher rate, pay a 42.4% higher average price, and place orders worth 71.3% more than shoppers who never see a Store. Those numbers come from Amazon, not from us, and they're the strongest argument for treating a Store as a conversion tool rather than a brand-safety checkbox.

A Worked Example: What a Store Actually Does to Order Value

Take a brand running Sponsored Brands ads with no Store behind them, averaging a $40 order. Using Amazon's own lift figure — orders from Store visitors run 71.3% higher — that same shopper's order becomes roughly $68 once the ad lands on a Store instead of a single product page. That's not a guarantee; it's what Amazon's aggregate data says happens when traffic hits a built-out storefront instead of one listing. The gap comes from cross-sell: a shopper who lands on a Store sees the rest of the catalog, not just the one SKU the ad was for.

Where this breaks down is reconciliation — matching which campaign sent the traffic that produced which lift, at scale, across dozens of ASINs and multiple Store pages. We built our own software, Orbit, instead of renting someone else's, specifically because this kind of cross-referencing between ad data and Store analytics is where most tools stop and most agencies stop looking. Across the $500M+ in Amazon spend we manage for more than 100 brands, mismatched Store and campaign data is one of the more common reasons a brand can't tell if their Store is actually working.

The Mistake Everyone Makes (Including One We've Made)

The most common mistake is building the Store before checking where the traffic to it will actually come from. A beautiful homepage with no plan to link it from Sponsored Brands campaigns, product page bylines, or external social just sits there, unvisited. The lift numbers above only apply to shoppers who reach the Store — a Store nobody finds produces nothing.

A mistake we've made ourselves: shipping a Store where the homepage looked strong but the navigation buried the exact product a Sponsored Brands ad had promised. The ad said one thing, the click landed the shopper three menus deep from what they clicked for, and conversion dropped instead of rising. The fix is boring but it works — every ad that points at a Store should point at the specific subpage that matches the ad's promise, not the homepage by default.

When the Store Won't Publish, or It's Live But Nothing Moved

If your Store submission gets rejected, check the obvious first: Brand Registry status has to be fully approved, not pending, and images or copy referencing competitors, guarantees, or unapproved claims will bounce every time. Amazon doesn't publish a fixed review turnaround, so if it's taking longer than expected, that's normal — there's no published SLA to hold them to.

If the Store is live but the lift never shows up, check whether traffic actually reaches it. A Store that only gets found through the brand byline on your own listings will convert the shoppers you already had. A Store that's linked from Sponsored Brands, from display, and from your own site traffic is the one that shows the numbers above. If none of your paid traffic points at it, the Store isn't broken — it's just never been tested.

Which one you should actually pick

Sellers just starting out need the account, not the Store — build the Store once there's a catalog and a trademark to register. Brands with traffic already flowing to single listings get the most from a Store, since the lift only shows up once ads and byline links actually point at it. Full Circle builds and manages Stores as part of full-service Amazon work, including publicly named accounts like HexClad, Ridge, and Beardbrand, but a brand with one SKU and no ad budget yet doesn't need an agency for this step — the Store Builder alone will do it.

Common questions

Do I need a seller account before I can create a Brand Store?

Yes. A Brand Store requires Brand Registry enrollment, and Brand Registry requires an existing seller or vendor account plus an active trademark. There's no way to build a Store first and figure out selling later.

How much does it cost to create an Amazon store?

It depends which one you mean. A Professional seller account costs $39.99 a month plus selling fees, per Amazon's own pricing page. A Brand Store itself is free to build and maintain — the cost sits in the seller account underneath it, not the storefront.

Can I create a Brand Store without inventory listed yet?

No. You need active listings and Brand Registry approval before the Store Builder becomes available. The Store is designed to showcase a catalog that already exists on Amazon, not to launch one.

How long does it take to get a Brand Store approved?

Amazon doesn't publish a fixed review time, so treat any specific number you see elsewhere as unofficial. Submission review typically follows building the pages, but the duration isn't guaranteed or stated by Amazon.

What's the actual difference between a Store and a regular product listing?

A listing sells one product on one page. A Store is a destination that shows your full catalog, brand story, and lifestyle content across multiple pages, reachable by URL, by the brand byline on any of your listings, or by linking ad campaigns directly to it.
