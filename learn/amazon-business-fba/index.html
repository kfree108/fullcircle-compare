The short answer

An Amazon FBA business is a company that sells physical products on Amazon while Amazon stores, packs, ships and handles returns through Fulfillment by Amazon. You pay a referral fee, a fulfillment fee and storage fees on every unit, on top of your product cost and advertising spend.

"Amazon FBA business" means three different things — know which one you're asking about

The phrase gets used loosely, and that's where most confusion starts. There's the fulfillment service itself — Amazon's warehouses picking, packing and shipping your orders. There's the company you build around it — the brand, the product, the listings, the ad account, the P&L. And there's the asset — an existing FBA operation bought and sold as a going concern, complete with inventory, historical sales data and Brand Registry.

Most people searching this term are somewhere between the second and third: they either want to start a company that sells through FBA, or they're looking at an FBA business for sale and trying to figure out what they'd actually be buying. Both are covered below, because the economics underneath them are the same fee structure.

How to start an Amazon FBA business — and the math you need before you order inventory

The mechanics are straightforward: pick a product, source it, register a Professional seller account, list the product, ship inventory to an Amazon fulfillment center, and let Amazon handle picking, packing and shipping from there. A Professional selling plan runs $39.99/month plus selling fees, on top of whatever you pay to make or import the product.

The part the sign-up pages gloss over is what happens to your margin once fees stack up. Say you're selling a $20 item. The referral fee for that category might run around 15%, roughly $3. The FBA fulfillment fee for a small, light item in that price range might land somewhere around $4–5, based on size tier and weight. Add storage — charged per cubic foot per month, higher in October through December — and before you've spent a cent on advertising, a large share of that $20 is already gone to fulfillment and referral fees alone.

Run the actual product through Amazon's own Revenue Calculator before you place the first inventory order, not after. The most common mistake at this stage isn't a bad product — it's ordering stock based on the sale price, then discovering the real margin once fees and ad spend are subtracted.

What it actually costs to run — the fee stages, not the marketing pitch

Every FBA business carries the same fee types, in the same order, regardless of category. None of these are fixed dollar amounts — Amazon publishes exact rates by category and size tier, and they change — but the structure below doesn't change often.

Buying or selling an Amazon FBA business

An Amazon FBA business for sale is usually a bundle, not a single asset: the entity that owns the seller account (accounts themselves aren't transferable under Amazon's terms — what actually changes hands is the LLC), the product listings and their sales history, Brand Registry, and existing inventory. Brokers typically price these as a multiple of trailing-twelve-month discretionary earnings — profit after cost of goods, fulfillment fees and advertising — not a multiple of revenue.

If you're the one trying to sell an Amazon FBA business, the number that sets your price isn't last month's top-line sales. It's twelve months of profit with advertising spend broken out clearly. A buyer who's done this before will ask for ACOS and TACoS trends, not just a revenue chart, because a listing ranking on unsustainable ad spend isn't worth what its revenue suggests.

The honest mistake sellers make here: leading with revenue growth in the listing and burying the ad spend that produced it. A business that grew 40% while its ad spend grew faster isn't a growth story — it's a business quietly losing money on its best-selling ASIN, and a buyer who checks the numbers will find that fast.

The mistake that actually kills FBA businesses

It's rarely the sourcing and rarely the ad account in isolation. It's treating advertising as separate from the catalog and inventory underneath it. A campaign can be structured well and still lose money if the listing has stockouts, wrong images for the category, or fees quietly eating margin nobody rechecked after a size-tier reclassification.

We've made this mistake ourselves: on early accounts we've optimized bids aggressively before confirming the product page converted at a rate that justified the spend. Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon spend across 100+ brands, and the accounts that stall are almost always the ones where advertising ran ahead of the catalog and supply chain work underneath it — not the ones where the ad account itself was mismanaged.

If your numbers look wrong — margin thinner than expected, ACOS climbing, sales flat despite more spend — check the fee side first. Is inventory aging past 181 days. Has a size-tier reclassification quietly raised your fulfillment fee. Is a competitor forcing your buy box price down. The fix is rarely just "spend more on ads."

Which one you should actually pick

Starting an FBA business suits someone with a sourced product and the patience to run the fee math before ordering inventory. Buying one suits someone who can read past the revenue line to discretionary earnings. Selling one rewards sellers who disclose ad spend honestly rather than lead with a growth number that advertising alone produced.

Common questions

Is an Amazon FBA business a real business, or is it just dropshipping?

It's different from dropshipping. In an FBA business, you own the product, hold inventory (Amazon's warehouse holds it physically, but you own it commercially), and carry the risk if it doesn't sell. Dropshipping means you never take title to the inventory at all. FBA is a fulfillment method layered on top of a normal retail business — sourcing, branding and inventory risk are still yours.

How much money do I need to start an Amazon FBA business?

There's no single number, because it depends on your product cost and order volume, but the fixed piece is public: a Professional selling account is $39.99/month plus selling fees. On top of that, budget for your first inventory order, per-unit referral and fulfillment fees, and an advertising budget — check Amazon's Revenue Calculator against your specific product before committing to a number.

Can you actually sell an Amazon seller account?

Not the account itself — Amazon's terms don't allow account transfers. What gets sold is the business entity (usually an LLC) that owns the account, along with its inventory, listings, sales history and Brand Registry. The buyer takes over the entity, not a login.

What's the difference between FBA and FBM?

FBA (Fulfilled by Amazon) means Amazon stores, ships and handles returns for you, and your listing is Prime-eligible by default. FBM (Fulfilled by Merchant) means you store and ship the product yourself, keeping more control and avoiding fulfillment fees, but you lose the Prime badge unless you qualify for Seller Fulfilled Prime.

What determines the price when an FBA business is sold?

Brokers and buyers generally price these deals as a multiple of trailing-twelve-month discretionary earnings — profit after cost of goods, Amazon fees and advertising — not a multiple of revenue. A business with strong top-line sales but thin or declining margin after ad spend will sell for less than the revenue number suggests.
