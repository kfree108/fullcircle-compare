How to Become an Amazon Seller (and What the Sign-Up Page Skips)

The short answer

You become an Amazon seller by registering an account (Individual or Professional, $39.99/month), verifying identity and bank details, listing a product, and choosing fulfillment. Signing up takes an afternoon. Building a listing that survives Amazon's fees and finds buyers takes considerably longer — that's the part the registration flow doesn't cover.

The actual steps, in order

Becoming an Amazon seller is a form, not a decision. You register, verify identity and banking, choose a plan, and list a product — Amazon can have you live within days if your documents line up. The decision that actually matters is what happens in the weeks after that, and the sign-up flow doesn't cover it at all.

To register, you'll need:

  • A government ID and a legal or business name that matches your other documents exactly
  • A bank account that can receive deposits
  • A chargeable credit card
  • Tax information — an SSN for a sole proprietorship or an EIN for a registered business
  • A phone number for two-step verification

Then you pick a plan. Individual has no monthly fee but charges a per-item fee on every sale and blocks advertising, bulk listing tools, and most Brand Registry benefits. Professional costs $39.99 a month plus selling fees and unlocks everything else — it's the plan almost every serious seller ends up on, usually within the first few months of testing.

What a sale actually costs you

Say you list a product at $30. Amazon takes a referral fee off the top — the rate depends on category, and most categories fall somewhere between the high single digits and high teens as a percentage of the sale price. If you use FBA, a separate fulfillment fee comes out based on the item's size and weight, plus storage while it sits in a warehouse. None of that touches advertising, which for most new listings isn't optional — it's how you get found on page one against listings that already have reviews and rank.

Run that math before you list, not after your first payout arrives smaller than expected. A $30 item that nets a comfortable margin after referral and fulfillment fees looks fine until you add the ad spend needed to actually generate the sale — now the margin question is real, and it's the question the registration page never asks.

We see this pattern at scale, not just on single SKUs. Full Circle has managed more than $500M in Amazon ad spend across 100+ brands, and the accounts that hold up in year two aren't the ones that found the lowest-fee category — they're the ones that budgeted for advertising as a real cost line before they ever listed a product, not as an afterthought once organic sales stalled.

Where new sellers go wrong

The advertising credit new accounts get is real money, and it gets wasted constantly. The common mistake — one we've watched happen repeatedly, and made an early version of ourselves — is spending it in the first week on broad-match keywords with no negative targeting, because a credit feels like free money. By the time there's enough data to learn anything from it, the credit is gone and you're paying full price to relearn the same lesson.

The other frequent mistake is picking a category at registration without checking what it locks in. Category affects the referral fee rate, search placement, and sometimes compliance requirements — batteries, supplements, and anything marketed for children carry extra documentation. Moving a live listing to a better-fitting category later is possible, but it rarely happens cleanly and can reset how that listing shows up in the new category's search.

A third mistake: staying on the Individual plan indefinitely because it feels cheaper. It's fine for testing a handful of items, but the per-item fee plus the lack of advertising access means most sellers who stay on it long-term are paying more per unit than they would on Professional, while getting less of the toolset that actually moves a listing.

When something doesn't match what you expected

Verification gets stuck far more often than approval gets denied outright. If your documents don't match exactly — a business name on your ID that differs from your bank statement by a middle initial, an address format that doesn't match your utility bill — Amazon's system stalls rather than rejects. The fix is boring: resubmit with an exact match, not a better explanation.

If your first payout is lower than you calculated, check the fee breakdown before assuming Amazon made an error. Misclassified categories, unexpected FBA storage fees, and referral fees calculated on item price plus shipping — not just the item price — account for most of the gap. Seller Central's payments report shows the line items; read it before you dispute anything.

If a listing gets suspended, the reflex is to resubmit immediately. Don't. Read the specific policy cited, gather the exact evidence it asks for — invoices, compliance certificates, corrected content — and submit once. Repeated vague appeals slow the review down; they don't speed it up.

Once you're approved, what actually decides growth

Registration decides whether you can sell. Everything after that — Brand Registry enrollment, a catalog structured so Amazon's search can actually parse it, fulfillment that doesn't run you out of stock during your first good week, advertising that spends toward a target instead of just spending — decides whether you sell profitably. Most of the incentives Amazon offers new sellers, from ad credits to cash back on branded sales to discounted freight, are built to get you through that first stretch. None of them solve it for you.

Common questions

Do I need an LLC to become an Amazon seller?

No. You can register as a sole proprietor using your SSN. An LLC or EIN isn't required to start, but it's worth setting up once you're carrying real inventory risk or want liability separation — and Brand Registry paperwork is easier when your registered business name matches your trademark exactly.

How long does Amazon seller approval take?

There's no fixed number Amazon publishes, and it varies enough that quoting one wouldn't hold up. Verification can clear in under a day if your ID, bank details, and business name match exactly across every document. It stalls — sometimes for weeks — on mismatches, not on your product or business type.

What's the real difference between the Individual and Professional plans?

Individual has no monthly fee but charges a per-item fee and blocks advertising and bulk tools. Professional costs $39.99 a month plus selling fees and unlocks the full toolkit, including Sponsored Products access. Most sellers planning to move more than a handful of items end up on Professional within months.

Can I sell on Amazon without holding inventory myself?

You can use Fulfillment by Amazon so Amazon holds and ships the inventory instead of you. You still need to be the seller of record with proper invoices — dropshipping arrangements where a third party ships under your listing without your involvement violate Amazon's policies and risk account suspension.

When does it make sense to bring in help instead of running this alone?

Usually once the questions shift from "how do I list a product" to "why isn't this profitable at scale" — ad spend that isn't converting, a catalog that's outgrown one person's attention, or fulfillment problems showing up as bad reviews. Early registration and single-SKU testing rarely need outside help.
