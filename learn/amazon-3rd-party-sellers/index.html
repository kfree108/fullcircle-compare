What Is an Amazon Third-Party Seller — And How Does the Model Actually Work?

The short answer

A third-party seller is any business selling on Amazon that isn't Amazon's own retail arm — shown as "Sold by" on the listing. They set their own prices, pay Amazon a referral fee plus fulfillment costs if using FBA, and compete with other sellers for the buy box on shared listings.

Third-party seller, in plain terms

A third-party seller is any business selling on Amazon that isn't Amazon's own retail operation. Check the "Sold by" line under the Buy Now button and you'll see one of three patterns: Sold by Amazon, Ships from Amazon (Amazon is the retailer), Sold by [Seller Name], Ships from Amazon (a third-party seller using Amazon's fulfillment network), or Sold by [Seller Name], Ships from [Seller Name] (the seller handles everything themselves).

Most listings on Amazon are run by third-party sellers, not Amazon's retail arm, which surprises people who assume anything on amazon.com is Amazon-owned inventory. Usually it isn't. The seller sets the price, manages the listing content, and owns customer service — unless Amazon fulfills the order, in which case Amazon takes over shipping and returns.

This matters whether you're buying or selling. Buyers need to know who to contact when something goes wrong. Brands need to know that "third-party seller" doesn't just describe outside resellers — if you sell your own products on Amazon through Seller Central rather than Vendor Central, you are a third-party seller too.

How the money actually moves: a worked example

Selling as a third party isn't free, and the fee structure catches a lot of first-time sellers off guard. Two charges apply to nearly every sale: a referral fee (a percentage of the sale price, set by category) and, if you use Fulfillment by Amazon, an FBA fulfillment fee based on size and weight, plus ongoing storage fees.

Here's how that plays out on a simple example. Say an item sells for $30. A referral fee in the mid-teens percentage range — common across a lot of categories — takes roughly $4.50 off the top before the seller sees a cent. Add an FBA pick-and-pack fee of a few dollars more, and the seller's real take before advertising, returns, or cost of goods can land closer to $20 than $30. None of this is hidden — Amazon publishes both fee schedules — but sellers who price against the $30 sticker instead of the net number find out the hard way that their margin was thinner than planned.

We've built pricing models like this across more than $500M in managed Amazon spend and 100+ brands, and the most common surprise in a first account audit is a seller who has never actually calculated their net-of-fees margin at the SKU level — they've been pricing off gut feel since launch.

FBA vs FBM: who fulfills the order changes everything

Every third-party seller chooses, listing by listing, whether Amazon or the seller handles fulfillment. The choice affects price, buy box odds, and how much operational work the seller carries day to day. Neither is automatically better — it depends on volume, margin, and how much control the seller wants over the shipping experience.

The buy box: why the same listing can have ten sellers

When more than one seller lists the same product — same barcode, same detail page — Amazon decides which seller's offer sits behind the main "Add to Cart" button. That's the buy box, and it shifts based on price, fulfillment speed, seller performance history, and stock levels, not just who's cheapest.

For a shopper this is invisible plumbing. For a brand, it can mean several unauthorized resellers competing for the buy box on your own product, sometimes dragging the price down right as your advertising is trying to hold it up. This is the part Amazon's own help pages don't cover, because from a buyer-support standpoint it's someone else's problem.

The mistake most brands make with third-party sellers

The most common mistake we see brand owners make is assuming every third-party seller on their listing is authorized. In practice, unauthorized resellers — sourcing through liquidation, retail arbitrage, or grey-market channels — show up on popular listings often enough that ignoring them is a decision, not neutral ground.

The mistake we've made ourselves: treating buy box and pricing monitoring as a set-it-once task after a brand launches, instead of a standing weekly check. A single listing content change or a new reseller can knock a brand out of buy box contention within days, and if nobody is watching, the first sign is usually a sales drop that already cost a week.

When it goes wrong: what to actually do

If you're a buyer with a problem — item never arrived, doesn't match the listing, seller gone quiet — start with the seller directly through Your Orders; sellers are expected to respond within 48 hours. If they don't, or the item shipped from Amazon and carries the Prime badge, Amazon customer service and the A-to-z Guarantee are the actual paths, not a general complaint email.

If you're a brand and the bad news is a lost buy box, a suspended listing, or a hijacked detail page, the fix isn't a support ticket — it's account-level diagnosis: confirm who currently holds the buy box and why (price, stock, fulfillment lag), check whether the listing content was changed without authorization, and file through Amazon's brand tools if a genuine violation — counterfeit goods, IP infringement, unauthorized use of your content — is happening. Guessing costs more time than the actual fix usually takes.

Which one you should actually pick

If you're buying and just need the mechanics of an order, Amazon's own help pages cover cancellations, refunds, and the A-to-z Guarantee well — you don't need anything more. If you're a brand trying to work out what third-party selling actually costs after fees, who's competing on your buy box, and why margin never matches the sticker price, that's a harder question, and it's the one this page is built to answer.

Common questions

Is it safe to buy from a third-party seller on Amazon?

Generally yes. Payment is processed through Amazon, not sent directly to the seller, and buyers are covered by the A-to-z Guarantee if something goes wrong. Watch the "Sold by / Ships from" line — if a seller ever asks you to pay them directly outside Amazon's checkout, that's outside Amazon's protection and worth reporting.

What's the actual difference between Amazon and a third-party seller?

Amazon as a seller means Amazon owns the inventory and is the retailer of record. A third-party seller is an independent business — could be the brand itself, could be a reseller — using Amazon's marketplace to sell, with Amazon acting as the platform and, optionally, the fulfillment provider.

Can a brand be a third-party seller of its own products?

Yes, and most are. Selling through Seller Central (rather than the invite-only Vendor Central) makes a brand a third-party seller of its own goods. It comes with more pricing control but also full responsibility for fees, listings, and customer service.

Why does my product listing show multiple third-party sellers?

Anyone can list against an existing barcode if they can source the product, authorized or not. Multiple sellers on one listing usually means resellers — sometimes authorized retailers, sometimes not — competing for the buy box, which can affect price and margin even if the brand never approved it.

How do I find out who actually fulfills an item before I buy it?

Check the text directly under the Buy Now button on the product page. It will read "Ships from Amazon" or "Ships from [Seller Name]" — that tells you who handles shipping and, usually, who to contact if there's a problem.
