Full service is a scope claim, and scope claims are checkable

Nothing stops an agency using the phrase. Plenty of firms that describe themselves this way run advertising with creative attached, which is a good business and a completely different purchase from the one you think you are making.

You can settle it in five minutes with no sales call. Open the agency's own services page, ignore everything about bidding and campaign structure, and look for four capabilities that have nothing to do with media:

  • Catalogue repair. Broken variation families, stranded inventory, duplicate ASINs, GS1 and UPC problems, brand name and category changes.
  • Account health and case work. Suppression appeals, listing reinstatement, policy correspondence, documentation gathering.
  • Supply and forecasting. Restock planning, cover targets, fee band and packaging work — the part that decides whether there is anything to advertise next month.
  • Creative production held in-house. Photography, video and A+ content made by people on the same team, not commissioned on request.

Two or more missing means you are looking at an advertising or creative specialist whatever the header says. That is not a fault and it is not a trick. It is a different product with a different cost base, and it should be priced and shortlisted as one. The only mistake is buying it while believing you have covered the other three.

A second check takes another minute: ask which of those four the agency has done in the last thirty days, on any client. Capability listed on a page and capability exercised this month are not the same claim.

Four things sit upstream of every ad you buy

The argument for breadth is not that more services are nicer. It is that four things determine the ceiling on advertising, and none of them can be moved from inside an ad account.

Catalogue integrity. A suppressed ASIN cannot be advertised at any bid. A variation family that has split leaves your reviews and your rank distributed across children that should have been one record, so every campaign pointed at it is buying traffic to the weaker half.

Conversion assets. Images, title, bullets, A+ content, price and review position decide what happens after the click. Advertising buys the click and has no vote on the rest. There is a hard version of this in our own pod checklists: we do not launch new campaigns on a product rated below 4.0 stars, because below four the traffic is bought at full price and converts at a discount. That is standing practice across our managed accounts rather than a market law, and it holds up often enough that we treat it as a gate.

Account health. Unresolved cases quietly cap everything downstream, and they are the single most common thing absent from an advertising-led scope.

Supply. A stockout costs rank, and rank has to be bought back at a worse rate than it was lost. A brand that runs out twice a year is funding its own inefficiency and calling it an advertising problem.

If all four are healthy, the case for full service collapses. That is the honest shape of the argument, and it is why this page spends as long on the exceptions as on the pitch.

Scope is really about who is allowed to act

Finding a problem and being able to fix it are separate capabilities. Most agencies sell the first and quietly depend on you for the second.

Here is a concrete one from our own work. On one account we found a product weighing 1.119 lb being billed as 2.00 lb, because the box measured 6.77 by 6.38 by 6.06 inches and triggered a dimensional weight of 1.883 lb. Getting under 1.75 lb was worth between $1.94 and $2.56 a unit, and on the best-selling variant that single fee band was the difference between roughly 20% margin and the 44 to 53% its siblings held. The entire gap was packaging. That was one account — a fishing tackle brand — not a benchmark, and we are telling it as a story rather than a typical result.

Notice what an advertising-only scope can do with that finding: write it in a report. Acting on it means a packaging change, a new box specification, a re-measurement request and a fee band that only moves at the next inbound shipment. Four functions, three of which sit outside the ad account.

The other half of this is data. Across our managed accounts, the most common blocker on a profitability project is not strategy at all — it is that cost-of-goods data has been blank for four or five years on part of the catalogue. Without landed cost per product, every margin conversation is estimation. Any agency promising profit improvement without naming which data it needs from you, and by when, is promising something it cannot deliver. Apply that test to us as readily as to anyone on your shortlist.

Breadth is bought for the bad weeks, not the good ones

In a calm quarter a full-service retainer looks like overhead. The scope earns itself in the weeks where something irreversible is happening and nobody with the right access is watching.

Three from our own accounts, told as single episodes rather than as things you should expect.

Removals do not wait for Monday. Amazon creates removal and disposal orders at weekends and they cannot be cancelled. On one account — a medical-device brand — 270 of 600 units were destroyed before Monday under a stale flag nobody had actioned, and it took a four-hour live-chat escalation to freeze the rest. The auto-removal queue is a Friday check in our operating routine now rather than a Monday one. This is the real answer to why Amazon accounts need daily attention: not because bids need daily tuning, but because irreversible things happen on the platform's schedule.

Appeals have mechanics nobody documents. Replying to your own appeal to chase it can restart the review from the beginning; support confirmed that to us privately and it appears nowhere a seller would look. What follows from it is specific practice — keep exactly one case identifier, request an internal review rather than filing a fresh appeal, and never send a reminder email into an open appeal.

Reinstatement is slow, then fragile. Getting four suppressed listings back took 40 to 45 days of escalation on one account, and the recovery afterwards had to be staged one change at a time — titles, then bullets, then secondary images, with observation between each. Sellers routinely undo a hard-won reinstatement in the first 48 hours by fixing everything at once.

None of that work produces a chart. It is also the work that decides whether the media budget has anywhere useful to go.

Three times you should not hire a full-service agency

We sell full service, so read this in the spirit it is offered. There are common situations where it is the wrong purchase and we would rather say so on a public page than four months into a contract.

One: your constraint is genuinely one function. Clean catalogue, conversion at category norm, reliable stock — then a full-service fee buys headcount you will not touch. Buy the function. Dr. PPC is ad management on its own at $300 a month plus 3% of ad spend, capped, month-to-month, with the first 30 days free. Use it when the ad account is the whole problem and you want the work without the relationship.

Two: you have capable people and no visibility. Some brands already employ a good operator and simply cannot see the account properly. That is a software gap, and paying a retainer to close it is an expensive way round. Orbit is what we built for that, and it comes with the productised services at no extra charge. Use it when the missing thing is a view of your own data, not a pair of hands.

If you are paying storage surcharges, low-inventory fees and placement fees while advertising a product that keeps going unavailable, media scope is not the answer. Put the scope in the contract, not in the pitch

Scope disputes almost never start as disputes. They start as an assumption nobody wrote down. Six clauses worth settling before signature, with any agency including this one.

  • Name the four upstream functions explicitly. Catalogue repair, case work, supply planning and creative production, each marked included, excluded or billed separately. A services page is marketing; a statement of work is the agreement.
  • Say which functions carry a service level. A case opened within one working day is a commitment; best efforts is not. Pick the two or three functions where slow response would cost you real money and get those written down.
  • Agree what gets reported when nothing visible happened. Compliance escalations and forecasting produce no artefact for weeks. Observed across our own client base, the most common reason a client loses confidence in an agency is not bad results — it is a false impression of inactivity while the work happens out of sight. So require the invisible work to appear in the report, and ask for the bad weeks first.
  • Set a decision window. Advertising reads over four to eight weeks; catalogue, creative and review work reads over a quarter. Write down which changes get judged on which clock.
  • Name the data you owe them. Landed cost per SKU, inventory positions, upcoming shipments. Put dates on it.
  • Settle exit before you settle price. Notice period, who holds the ad account and the brand store, what documentation you receive, and how quickly access transfers.

Which one you should actually pick

Buy full service when catalogue, conversion, account health and supply are all leaking at once, because acting on any finding needs authority in the other three. Buy a single function when only one is broken, buy software when you have people and no visibility, and hire a multi-channel agency when Amazon is a minority of your revenue.

What to do with this

The right pick depends on how many hours a week the account will actually get. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. If nobody has four to ten hours a week to work that list, buy the work rather than the software.

Common questions

What counts as a genuinely full-service Amazon agency?

Check the services page for four capabilities unrelated to advertising: catalogue repair, account health and case management, supply or forecasting work, and creative production held in-house. If two or more are absent you are looking at an advertising or creative specialist regardless of the label. Then ask which of the four they have actually exercised in the last thirty days.

Is full service more expensive than an advertising agency?

Usually, because the headcount is wider. Whether it is more expensive per unit of outcome depends entirely on where your ceiling sits. With a clean catalogue and reliable stock, full service is worse value than a specialist. Without them, a specialist is optimising inside a limit it has no authority to move.

Can I use a full-service agency and keep advertising in-house?

Yes, and it is a sensible structure when you have a strong internal media operator. Agree in writing who owns campaign structure, who may change budgets, and which single report both sides argue from. Split ownership of an ad account without those three written down is where the arrangement usually fails, and it fails slowly enough that nobody notices for a quarter.

How long before wider scope shows up in the numbers?

Advertising changes read within weeks. Catalogue, creative and account-health work reads over a quarter or more, because it depends on Amazon reindexing, review accumulation and stock cycles. Treat any promise of a fast transformation on the second and third of those with caution — the mechanics simply do not move that quickly, whoever is doing the work.

Does a full-service agency handle suspensions and appeals?

Some do and some route it to a partner, so ask directly and ask for a recent example. The practical detail worth testing is whether they know the mechanics: one case identifier rather than repeated fresh appeals, internal review requests, staged recovery after reinstatement. An agency that describes appeals as simply escalating to support has not done many.

A real look at your account, not a pitch deck.
