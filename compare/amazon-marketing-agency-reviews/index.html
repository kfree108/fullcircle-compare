The short answer

Amazon marketing agency reviews are shaped by who writes them, and three groups almost never do: clients who left quietly, enterprise buyers bound by procurement policy, and everyone the agency chose not to invite. Reconstructing that missing population matters more than reading the reviews that exist.

Where this comes from

Full Circle is an Amazon agency, so we are a competing party writing about how to assess competing parties. Weigh it accordingly. We also have no public review corpus of our own — no Clutch profile, no Trustpilot listing, no invited programme — which is a genuine hole in our record and one you should hold against us rather than treat as modesty.

It does mean we are not defending a score, and it means everything below can be a method rather than a pitch. All the profile readings are from 20 August 2026 and are listed at the foot of the page. None of them is a judgement about any firm's quality, and none should be read as one.

Why marketing work is the hardest thing to review honestly

Before the selection problem, there is an attribution problem, and it is specific to this scope.

When an agency does one narrow thing — a suspension appeal, an EDI integration, a Vendor Central migration — the reviewer can say whether it worked, because the outcome is binary and visible. Amazon marketing is not like that. It is listings, images, A+ content, brand store, advertising, creative, sometimes pricing and promotions, delivered simultaneously into a market that is also moving on its own. Sales went up. Something in that list caused it, or the category grew, or a competitor stocked out, or a review count crossed a threshold, or the season turned.

So a five-star review of a marketing engagement is a genuine report of a good experience and a weak report of a causal claim. That is not the reviewer's failing; it is the honest limit of what anyone in that seat can know. It is why a review that describes a specific mechanism — a catalogue restructure, a negative-keyword build, a variation fix that unified reviews across a family — is worth ten reviews that describe a growth number, and why you should read for mechanism rather than for outcome.

The three populations that never write a review

The client who left quietly. Ending an agency relationship is usually undramatic: the contract lapses, the work moves in-house, the budget gets reallocated. Nobody is angry enough to post and nobody is happy enough either. This is the single largest missing group and it is the one you would most benefit from hearing, because it contains the answer to the only question that matters — what does the median engagement look like after eighteen months.

The enterprise buyer. Large consumer-goods companies are frequently barred by policy from endorsing suppliers publicly. That is why some firms with impressive named-client shelves carry almost no public reviews. It looks like an absence of evidence and it is actually an artefact of procurement rules. One firm in this category lists brands like Arm & Hammer, Burt's Bees, Polaroid and UTZ on its site while its directory profile carries two reviews. Nothing about that is contradictory.

Everyone who was not invited. On the nominated platforms, the agency chooses which client an analyst speaks to. That is the platform's design rather than a trick, and every listed firm operates the same way — but it means the corpus is a curated sample, and a distribution like forty-one five-star entries against one four-star entry tells you about the invitation process more than about the firm.

Add those three together and the visible corpus is drawn from a minority of a firm's relationships, weighted towards the successful and the recent. That is the material you are being asked to decide on.

Five questions that reconstruct the missing half

You cannot summon the reviews that were never written. You can ask for the same information directly, and how a firm handles these questions is itself the answer:

  • What is your client retention rate, and what is the denominator? Retention percentages are meaningless without the base. Ask how many clients were live twelve months ago, how many are live now, and whether accounts that shrank to a minimum fee count as retained.
  • What is the median engagement length? Not the longest, not the average — averages are dragged upwards by a handful of long-tenure accounts. The median tells you the typical experience.
  • Give me a reference whose engagement ended. This is the request that separates firms. A confident agency has two or three former clients who parted on good terms and will say so. A refusal is not proof of anything, but it is information.
  • What is the most recent account you lost, and why? A firm that answers this concretely — wrong fit, budget, an internal hire, a mistake they made — is showing you how it handles fault. A firm that has never lost an account for any reason it can articulate has not examined its own book.
  • What has triggered a fee change for an existing client in the past twelve months, and how much written notice would I get? This one came from the single four-star review in an otherwise uniformly five-star corpus: a client who was pleased with the work and surprised by an increase. It is a mild, specific and entirely believable complaint, it is the most useful data point in that whole record, and it points at something you can fix contractually before it happens. Sixty or ninety days' written notice is a normal ask and most firms will agree. Get written notice of fee changes from any agency, including us.

Then bring your own evidence to the table

The strongest position in an agency conversation is arriving with a number the agency did not give you. It is easier to get than most brands assume — one export, ninety days, one pivot table.

Here is the shape of what it can look like, from a named account we are cleared to discuss. On Walkize, ad spend of $267,131 produced $601,614 in attributed sales at a 44.4% ACOS. Within that, $89,885 — 33.6% of everything spent — went to search terms that returned no orders at all, spread across 89,045 separate search terms, while the top 1% of terms carried 75.5% of all sales. The account ran 30,921 unique search terms and 244,510 clicks at a blended $1.09 cost per click. One account, one period. We are not presenting it as a portfolio result.

Run the same export on your own account and take the zero-order figure into every conversation on your shortlist. It does two things. It gives you a concrete brief instead of a vague one, and it lets you score candidates on whether they can see what you already know is there. A firm that reads ninety days of your data and does not find your version of that number has told you something no review could.

Reading the corpus that does exist, quickly

None of this means ignore reviews. It means spend your time in the right fields. Four passes, roughly twenty minutes:

  • Sort by date and discard anything older than eighteen months unless you are specifically checking longevity. Agencies rehire and restructure; a 2021 review describes a different pod.
  • Check the entity has not renamed. This category renames often — one well-known Amazon firm relaunched under a new name in September 2022, and its older reviews sit under a name it no longer trades as. Ninety seconds of checking prevents an hour of reading about a predecessor.
  • Segment by service. A firm can be excellent at listings and ordinary at advertising, and the blended star average is designed to hide exactly that. On the nominated platforms the service tags are published on every entry, so this pass is mechanical.
  • Segment by project size band. If the strong entries cluster in one band and the weaker ones in another, you have found the firm's real operating range, which tells you whether you are the client it is built for.

Then read the replies to any critical review. How a firm answers in public is a free preview of how a dispute with you would go, and it is the one part of a review corpus the firm cannot curate after the fact.

Who we would hand a brief to, and where we would keep it

Read for mechanism and the existing corpora point somewhere useful. Reviewers describing Vendor Central purchase orders, shortage claims and chargeback recovery point to a 1P specialist, and that is a genuinely hard discipline most Amazon agencies decline. A firm publishing suspension appeal and reinstatement as a front-page service is the right call when the account is suppressed and the clock is the constraint. A firm whose declared service mix is mostly search engine optimisation is the right call when Amazon is one of four channels and one accountable vendor across all of them beats depth in any one. A firm built around cross-border marketplaces, VAT and localisation is the right call when the next dollar comes from a new country — we would not compete for that brief.

We would keep the brief where advertising, listings, catalogue and supply chain have to move as one decision, which is a narrower condition than the phrase full service suggests: it only matters when acting on a finding in one function needs authority over the other three. Which one you should actually pick

Amazon marketing agency reviews are a curated minority of each firm's relationships, weighted towards the recent and the successful, and the scope makes causal claims unreliable even when the reviews are sincere. Read for mechanism, segment by service and size band, then reconstruct the missing half by asking for churn, median tenure and a reference that ended — from us as readily as from anyone else.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — on the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.

Common questions

Why do almost all Amazon marketing agencies have high review scores?

Because of who writes reviews and who gets invited to. Clients who leave quietly rarely post, enterprise buyers are often barred by procurement policy from endorsing suppliers, and on nominated platforms the agency selects which client an analyst speaks to. The result is a curated sample weighted towards success, which is why ratings cluster high and cannot separate candidates.

What is the single most useful question to ask an agency?

Ask for a reference whose engagement ended. It is the request that separates firms, because a confident agency has former clients who parted on good terms and will say so. Follow it with the median engagement length, not the average — averages are pulled upward by a few long-tenure accounts and hide what a typical relationship looks like.

Should I trust a review that reports a big growth number?

Treat it as a genuine report of a good experience and a weak causal claim. Amazon marketing delivers listings, creative, advertising and promotions at once into a market that is also moving, so no reviewer can isolate the cause. Reviews describing a specific mechanism — a catalogue restructure, a negative-keyword build, a variation fix — carry far more information.

How recent does a review need to be?

Weight the last twelve to eighteen months heavily and everything older lightly. You are hiring a pod — a strategist, a daily analyst, an account contact — not a logo, and staff turnover means an older review describes people who may have moved on. Also confirm the firm has not renamed or changed hands since the review was written, which happens often in this category.
