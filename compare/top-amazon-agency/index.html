The short answer

Nobody independently ranks Amazon agencies. There is no regulator, no audited performance measure and no way to compare two agencies without access to both accounts. Every ranked list you find is published by a market participant. Use the lists for names, then run your own tests.

Our conflict, first

Full Circle is an Amazon management company. A ranked list of the best Amazon agencies, written by an Amazon agency, that happens to end with that agency, is worth nothing to you, and we are not going to publish one.

What is worth publishing is how the results page in front of you was assembled and what its ranking signal actually is, because once you can see the machinery you can read any of these lists — this one included — for the part that survives the conflict. The names survive. The order does not.

What a genuine ranking would require, and why none exists

Think about what it would take to rank two Amazon agencies honestly against each other.

You would need results from both, in comparable categories, at comparable revenue, over the same period, adjusted for the state of each account on day one. You would need the counterfactual — what those accounts would have done without the agency — which requires a holdout nobody ran. You would need the denominator behind every percentage, and you would need permission from clients to publish it. And you would need someone independent, funded by neither side, to audit the lot.

None of those conditions is close to being met, and there is no body attempting it. There is no licensing regime for Amazon agencies, no standard reporting definition, and no obligation on anyone to disclose an account they lost.

The absence is not a scandal. It is a structural fact about a young service category, and it is the same in most marketing disciplines. What it means practically is that every ranking you will read is a proxy: a proxy for who published, who paid, who optimised for the search term, or who asked their happiest clients for a review this quarter. Proxies are not worthless. They are just not the thing they look like, and the whole skill in this search is knowing which proxy you are holding.

Even the benchmarks people rank against are softer than they look. Across our managed portfolio, healthy accounts sit roughly between 8.7% and 21% total advertising cost of sale, and the number that matters is not the level but which direction it is being driven deliberately. A brand comparing itself to a single published good number is comparing itself to somebody else's stage, category and objective at once. If a single figure cannot rank two accounts, it certainly cannot rank two agencies.

Four kinds of list, and what each one is optimising for

Agency-published roundups. The most common result for this query. Canopy Management publishes a top-ten guide to Amazon advertising agencies; SalesDuo publishes a comparison of Amazon PPC agencies; Darkroom publishes a best-performance-marketing piece. All three are useful writing and all three are marketing by the publisher. When a publisher appears in its own ranking that is the format working as designed, not a scandal — but it does explain where the top slot came from.

Directories. Clutch, DesignRush and their equivalents mix verified client reviews with sponsored placement and paid profile upgrades. The reviews are often long, specific and attributed, which is more than most platforms manage. The ordering is a different mechanism from the reviews, and the firmographic fields — hourly band, minimum project size, headcount — are typed in by the agency itself.

Affiliate roundups. Written by publishers earning a referral fee on the introduction. Sometimes excellent research, always paid on the click. Check whether a disclosure exists at all.

Vendor comparison pages. Written by software companies selling against the agencies listed. These frequently carry startlingly precise fee figures for firms that publish none. Treat any number about a company that did not come from that company as unverified — we have watched market-range guides get relabelled as an agency's rate card often enough to know how the error travels.

What a partner badge or an award actually certifies

Two substitutes for a ranking get treated as if they were one, and they certify very different things.

Platform partner status is the more useful of the two. Amazon maintains its own partner directory, and appearing in it is an independent check that somebody outside the agency has verified something about them. It is a low bar rather than a quality ranking — it says a firm meets criteria, not that it will do well on your catalogue — but it is free to check and it is not self-reported. One caution: partner criteria are revised periodically, so check Amazon's own pages for what a given tier currently requires rather than trusting a description on an agency site that may be a year old.

Industry awards are almost always entered rather than won by measurement. A team writes a submission, selects the account, chooses the metric and pays a fee. Good work does win them and there is nothing improper about the process, but an award tells you an agency has a marketing function and a case study, not that its median client outperforms.

The same dating problem applies to both, and to reviews. This category has moved fast: firms have been acquired, rebranded, folded into holding companies and rebuilt around new platforms in the last three years. A badge, an award or a review from 2023 may describe an organisation that no longer exists in that form. Date every signal before you weigh it, and if a firm has changed ownership since, ask who from the original team is still there.

Reading a directory rating without over-reading it

Review platforms are worth reading and worth reading carefully, because two profiles showing the same score can rest on completely different evidence.

  • Sample size before score. A five-star average across a single review is a data point, not a track record. For a firm with hundreds of engagements it tells you almost nothing in either direction. Always read the count and the score together; a score without its count is not a fact.
  • Who was invited. Most agency reviews exist because the agency asked a happy client to leave one. That is normal practice rather than manipulation, but it skews every distribution upward and means the text carries far more information than the average.
  • Read for specifics. Canopy Management's Clutch record sits at 4.9 out of 5 across 35 reviews and clusters tightly around advertising, creative and weekly cadence. Thrive's sits at 4.6 across 109 reviews spanning every service it sells, not only Amazon. Those two facts tell you what each firm is for. The two numbers on their own do not.
  • Absence is not evidence. Several capable firms have empty or near-empty profiles, ourselves included. Some never ran a review programme, and some are too new. Where the public record is thin, replace it with references rather than treating silence as a verdict.
  • Verify the number where you can. Search snippets and aggregator sites have contradicted the live page badly enough, often enough, that we now treat a rating as unverified until it has been read on the platform that issued it.

Three tests that replace the ranking you cannot get

Since no external body will rank the candidates for you, run the ranking yourself. These three separate a shortlist faster than any list will.

The denominator test. Take any percentage a firm publishes and ask for the starting point, the period and the account's condition on day one. A 240% lift on a campaign restarted from near zero and a 240% lift on an established line are different achievements. Any agency proud of the work has both numbers to hand; the question is not hostile and the reaction to it is informative.

The deliverable test. Ask to see a real strategy document written for a real client, redacted. Then ask who read it before it was sent and what they changed. We learned the value of that question the hard way: a client returned a document of ours with the observation that it read like an audit nobody had read end to end, and that the issues in it should have been caught in the account weeks earlier rather than written up as findings. Both halves were correct, and it changed how we work — analysis a live account manager should have acted on in real time does not belong in a quarterly document at all. That was one client, told as a story. The question travels to every firm on your list.

The non-branded test. Ask how the firm reports branded and non-branded performance separately, and what the target is for each. Blended advertising cost of sale is the metric most likely to end an agency relationship, because it can look excellent for a year while non-branded growth stops entirely — a healthy branded harvest subsidises the average and the account coasts on demand it did not create. Then the question arrives, and there is no answer in the reporting. That pattern is a composite we hear constantly rather than one client's complaint, and the fix is separate budgets and separate efficiency targets per lane, with category impression share as the non-branded scoreboard.

Which one you should actually pick

There is no independent ranking of Amazon agencies, so treat every list as a name-collection tool and read the substitutes for what they certify rather than what they imply. Build a shortlist of three, date every signal, and decide on the denominator test, a redacted deliverable and how each firm reports non-branded growth separately.

What to do with this

The right pick depends on how many hours a week the account will actually get. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. If nobody has four to ten hours a week to work that list, buy the work rather than the software.

Common questions

Is there a genuine ranking of the top Amazon agencies?

No independent, audited ranking exists. There is no regulator, no standard performance definition, and no way to compare two agencies without access to both accounts and a counterfactual neither ran. What exists is directories mixing paid and earned signals, and roundups written by market participants. Gather names widely, then verify each one yourself.

Does Amazon partner status mean an agency is good?

It means an outside party has verified that a firm meets a set of criteria, which is more than a self-reported directory field and less than a quality judgement. It is worth checking because it is free and independent. Criteria are revised periodically, so read Amazon's own pages for what a tier currently requires rather than an agency's description of it.

Are Clutch ratings for Amazon agencies trustworthy?

The individual reviews generally are — long, specific and attributed. What deserves caution is treating a score or a ranked position as a like-for-like measure. Profile fields such as headcount, hourly band and minimum project size are self-reported, averages built on very few reviews carry almost no information, and older reviews may describe a firm that has since changed hands.

Should I hire the agency at the top of the list?

Only if it survives your own tests. Position correlates with who published, who paid and who optimised for the search term, not with who will do well on your catalogue. The firm that finds something specific in your live account in fifteen minutes is a far stronger signal than any ranking, and running that test on three finalists costs you nothing.

What if an agency has no public reviews at all?

Treat absence as missing data rather than a verdict, then replace it: ask for two references at your revenue and in your category, ask for a redacted deliverable, and ask who on the current team worked on the case study they showed you. Several capable firms simply never ran a review programme, and some are too new to have one.
