What we could verify, and what nobody here publishes

We checked directly rather than trusting a roundup. On 21 August 2026, acadia.io carries no rate card, no tier table and no minimum; the pricing path returns a not-found response and the site's routes end at contact forms. That is a finding, and it is a neutral one. Most full-service Amazon agencies price the same way, ourselves included — Full Circle publishes no agency rate card either, and saying so is more useful to you than pretending the category is uniformly opaque.

Because it is not. A handful of firms do print numbers, and the contrast is the most useful thing on this page. Darkroom publishes a full per-service rate card — fourteen lines, in US dollars, read on their own page on 21 August 2026 — with Amazon Management at $6,000 per month, Retail Media Management at $2,500 per month and Paid Media Management at $5,000 per month. Thrive publishes no Amazon rate but does state no long-term commitments on its Amazon page, which is rarer than it should be and worth more to some buyers than a headline figure.

So the honest framing is not "agencies hide their prices". It is that a published rate card exists in this market, which means you can walk into an Acadia conversation with a real external reference point rather than a feeling.

One standing caution about any agency figure you find in a listicle: check whether it is the price they charge or the price they say the market charges. Several agencies publish industry pricing guides on pages that look exactly like pricing pages, and reprinting those as a firm's rate card is a false statement about that firm.

The four lines inside a full-service agency invoice

Whatever the headline number is, an agency quote is assembled from up to four components, and brands routinely negotiate the one that matters least.

  • A base management fee. Fixed monthly, usually banded by scope or by the number of retailers in play. This is the number people negotiate hardest and it is rarely the largest line at scale.
  • A variable component. Usually a percentage — of ad spend, of channel revenue, or of gross merchandise value. This is the line that decides your bill as you grow, and the one most likely to be described vaguely on a call.
  • Production and creative. Photography, video, A+ modules, store builds. Frequently a separate schedule with its own rates.
  • Analysis and clean-room work. Amazon Marketing Cloud queries, incrementality design, custom reporting. Sometimes included, sometimes an add-on, almost never specified in the first proposal.

The variable line deserves a sentence of arithmetic. At $100,000 of monthly ad spend, one undisclosed percentage point is $12,000 a year — larger than most of the base-fee negotiations that consume the first three calls. We charge a percentage of spend on our own products and we are not going to argue against the model; it is how the category leader in software prices too. What we would argue for is that the percentage is named, capped where possible, and calculated on something you can define in a sentence.

One specific version of that, learned the hard way on our own paper. A client — a personal-care brand — challenged being charged a percentage of gross Amazon revenue rather than net, on the grounds that gross includes refunds and returns and they were therefore paying a fee on their own returns. They were right. We changed our contracts to a net basis. That is one client and one contract rather than a category rule, and it is a fair question to put to Acadia, to us, and to anyone else quoting you.

Two floors nobody can negotiate away

Before you compare agency fees, know the two costs that sit underneath every quote and move for nobody.

Amazon's own managed-service floor. Amazon DSP has no self-service minimum, but its managed service carries a $50,000 minimum spend as published on Amazon's own advertising pages. If display is in your plan, that number sets the shape of the conversation before an agency fee is discussed at all.

A minimum below which display is the wrong purchase. Our own standing guidance to prospects is that below roughly $10,000 to $15,000 a month we tell brands to put the money somewhere other than Amazon DSP. That is guidance we give, not a benchmark for the industry, and it is the sort of thing worth asking any agency to state plainly before you buy a capability you cannot feed.

Both of these are more decision-relevant than a few hundred dollars of retainer, and neither will appear in a fee comparison spreadsheet unless you put it there.

The pattern it exposes is simple and it runs both ways. A flat monthly fee is the cheaper invoice at high spend and the more expensive one at low spend — a published $6,000 monthly rate is a large bill against $15,000 of media and a modest one against $250,000. A percentage structure does the opposite: small early, and unbounded later unless it is capped. Our own Dr. PPC pricing is $300 a month plus 3% of ad spend with a cap, so it behaves like a percentage until the cap and like a flat fee afterwards.

Neither shape is virtuous. What matters is which one matches the direction you are travelling, and whether you have modelled the fee at the spend you will actually be at in eighteen months rather than the spend you are at today. Ask for that as a table, not a reassurance.

Two further checks worth running on any vendor, including us. Divide the advertised annual total by twelve and see whether it matches the monthly figure you were quoted; if it does not, you are looking at two different billing bases. And ask for written notice of fee changes, from any agency including us.

The cost that decides more than the fee does

Agency fee comparisons are a smaller variable than almost everyone treats them as, because the media budget dwarfs them and the media budget is where the waste sits.

From an account we are cleared to name: Walkize spent $267,131 on Amazon advertising against $601,614 of attributed sales. The headline was a 2.25x return at a 44.4% cost of sale — unremarkable, arguable, the sort of number that survives a quarterly review. Underneath it, $89,885 — 33.6% of everything spent — had gone to search terms that produced zero orders, across 89,045 individual terms, while the top 1% of terms carried 75.5% of all sales. That is 30,921 unique terms and 244,510 clicks at a $1.09 blended cost-per-click. One named account, from our own data. Not a typical result and not a promise about yours.

Put that next to the fee arithmetic. A brand at that spend arguing an agency down by $1,000 a month is chasing $12,000 a year while a third of the media budget is unexamined. Both are worth doing. Only one of them is worth doing first.

A related trap on the other side of the ledger, from our own accounts rather than a study: we judge every spend change on the cost of sale of the money we added, not on the account's blended figure. A blended number can look excellent for a year while non-branded growth stops entirely, and the fee conversation that follows is usually about the wrong thing.

Where Acadia earns a premium, and where we would send you elsewhere

Acadia is a broad growth agency — retail media across Amazon, Instacart, Walmart, Target, Kroger and Chewy, plus paid media, analytics, content and search, with named case work on its own site including TRUBAR, Fresh Thyme and European Wax Center. If your brief genuinely spans several retail media networks with brand media attached, a single firm holding all of it is worth paying for, and a specialist would need partners to cover the same ground. That is a real argument for a higher number, and we would not pretend otherwise.

Full Circle is a full-service Amazon management company with $500M+ in managed Amazon spend across 100+ brands. Our software products came out of this agency, which is why the account data drives the tooling rather than the other way round. We publish no agency rate card either — on transparency specifically, Darkroom is ahead of both of us.

Three cases where buying a product beats buying an agency:

  • You want the ad function run, not a relationship. Dr. PPC — $300 a month plus 3% of ad spend, capped, month-to-month, first 30 days free, Orbit included at no extra cost.
  • Display is the named line. reMKTR buys Amazon DSP on live seats as a service, and Dr. DSP is the same capability packaged as a product. Neither is the answer while sponsored search is leaking a third of its budget.
  • The margin problem is fees and stock. Dr. Stock covers inventory, fees and supply chain, quoted on a demo rather than published — which means a competitor with a rate card is ahead of us there too.

Which one you should actually pick

Acadia quotes on a call, like most full-service Amazon agencies including us. Pay their premium when your brief genuinely spans several retail media networks and needs brand media alongside. Use Darkroom's published rate card as your external reference point, insist the variable percentage is named and capped, and settle whether it is charged on gross or net before anyone talks about the base fee.

What to do with this

Before you compare subscription prices, pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders. On the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Whatever you buy — a seat, a service, or nothing — that number is the one it has to move, and a cheaper tool nobody has time to drive will not move it.

Common questions

How much does Acadia cost?

Acadia does not publish a price and its pricing path returns a not-found page, so any figure you see quoted elsewhere is somebody's estimate rather than their rate card. Ask for the fee broken into base management, variable percentage, production and analysis, and ask what each is calculated on.

Is a percentage of ad spend a fair way to buy agency work?

It is the standard structure in this category and we charge it ourselves, so we are not going to argue against it. The questions worth asking are whether the percentage is named, whether it is capped, and whether it is calculated on gross or net revenue. An uncapped percentage on a growing account compounds quietly.

Which Amazon agencies publish their prices?

Darkroom publishes a full per-service rate card in US dollars, including Amazon Management at $6,000 per month, read on their own page on 21 August 2026. Most others — including Acadia, Tinuiti, Canopy and Full Circle — quote on a call. Stated neutrally: it is the category norm, not a red flag.

Is there a minimum spend before Amazon DSP makes sense?

Amazon's own managed service carries a $50,000 minimum spend, published on Amazon's advertising pages, while self-service through a partner has no platform minimum. Separately, our own standing guidance to prospects is that below roughly $10,000 to $15,000 a month the money is better spent elsewhere.

What should I compare before I compare agency fees?

Your own search-term report for the last 90 days. Total the spend against terms that produced no orders. On one named account of ours that was 33.6% of everything spent. Whatever you buy — an agency, a product, or nothing — that is the number it has to move.
