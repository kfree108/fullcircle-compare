Disclosure first, because it changes how you should read this

Full Circle competes with Canopy Management. We sell against them, we lose briefs to them, and we would be pleased if this page brought you to us instead. Read it knowing that, and check every claim against the sources listed at the bottom.

The rules we set ourselves for this page: everything critical comes from Canopy's own reviewers on Clutch or from what is visibly absent on their own pages. We have not invented a weakness, we have not softened a strength, and where a number is self-reported we say so — including when it is ours.

Said plainly: Canopy is good at what they are good at, and on a fair number of briefs we would tell a brand to call them rather than us. There is a section near the bottom naming exactly which briefs those are.

What the reviews actually say

Their Clutch profile, read on 20 August 2026, shows 4.9 out of 5 across 35 reviews, an employee band of 50 to 249, headquarters in Austin, Texas, a listed hourly range of $150 to $199, and a $1,000+ minimum project size.

The praise clusters tightly, which in review data is a better signal than the average. Reviewers repeatedly cite sponsored-ads and DSP execution, responsiveness, and a reliable weekly meeting cadence with items delivered when promised. Several describe being treated as a partner rather than an account number. When positive reviews all point at the same capability rather than scattering into generic warmth, the capability is usually real.

Two disciplines to apply before you weigh any of that, and they apply to every agency profile you will read this week:

  • Sort by date and read the most recent dozen. An average is a number about history. Agencies change leadership, service mix and staffing, and a review from three years ago describes a company that may not exist in that form.
  • Do not average a small sample in either direction. Thirty-five reviews is a meaningful body of evidence; two is not. Where a profile is thin, state the count and decline the conclusion rather than dressing it as a verdict.

The hourly band and minimum project size on a directory profile are self-reported fields, not quotes. They tell you roughly which market a firm thinks it is in. They are not a proposal and nobody is bound by them.

Where they are genuinely strong

  • Advertising and DSP. This is the core and the review record supports it. If your catalogue is clean and your ceiling is media efficiency, this is the right kind of specialist to be talking to.
  • In-house creative. Product photography and video sit on their service list rather than being subcontracted, which shortens the loop between an advertising finding and a creative fix. On Amazon that loop length matters more than most buyers expect — a main image insight that takes six weeks to reach a shoot is worth very little.
  • Communication cadence. The single most consistent theme across their reviews, and the thing brands most often say is missing elsewhere. Underrated in every agency comparison and overwhelmingly what determines whether a relationship survives a bad quarter.
  • Adjacent revenue work. Revenue reclaim, inventory management, customer service management and full-service Walmart management all appear on their service list, which is broader than a pure advertising shop.

If you are shortlisting on advertising execution alone, they belong on the list. We would not argue otherwise on a call and we are not going to argue otherwise on a page.

The limit their own reviewers describe

This is the part of a page like this where a competitor usually invents something. We would rather quote their clients.

The recurring note in the Clutch record is that "CANOPY Management's core competencies lie in advertising, and they provide limited support in operational or logistical areas," with the observation that clients wanting broader support may need additional resources. That is their reviewers' language, not ours. A few reviewers also asked for more detailed dashboards, fewer acronyms in reporting, and an interface for real-time progress tracking. A small minority scored cost lower than the other categories.

Treat every line of that as fit information rather than failure. An advertising specialist that is described as an advertising specialist is behaving correctly. It is more useful than a generalist claiming everything, and it is exactly the disclosure a buyer needs.

But read it against your own situation. If your actual problem is a suppressed listing, a stalled Seller Support case, a broken variation family, or a restock cycle that keeps costing you rank and then charging you to buy it back, their own clients are telling you something you should act on. That is not a criticism of Canopy. It is the correct use of a review.

How to read agency headline numbers — including ours

Canopy's site carries several: $3.2B+ in managed revenue, 1,000+ brands scaled, 84% average year-over-year profit growth for partners, and a 99.1% partner retention rate. Their Clutch profile has referenced slightly different figures for profit growth and retention. These are self-reported and unaudited, as agency numbers across this category generally are, and small discrepancies between two of a company's own sources are a thing to ask about rather than to conclude anything from.

Three questions defuse any agency headline number, ours included:

  • What is the denominator? An average across what population, of what size?
  • Over what period? A retention rate measured over twelve months and one measured over four years are different claims wearing the same percentage.
  • Does the average include the accounts that left? Survivor-only averages are the most common way a true number tells a false story, and almost nobody asks.

So here is ours with the denominator attached. Full Circle is a full-service Amazon management company with $500M+ in managed spend across 100+ brands, including publicly named work with HexClad, Ridge, BK Beauty, Beardbrand, Epic Gardening and The Woobles.

And rather than an average, a single named account. On Walkize, $267,131 in ad spend produced $601,614 in attributed sales — 2.25x at 44.4% ACOS — while $89,885, or 33.6% of all spend, went to search terms that produced no orders whatsoever, and the top 1% of terms carried 75.5% of all sales. One account, read from the account. Not a portfolio claim and not a promise about yours.

The ranking guide they publish, and the one you are reading now

Canopy publishes a top-ten guide to Amazon advertising management agencies. Canopy is ranked first in it.

That is not a scandal, and it should not lower your opinion of them, because it is the standard behaviour of the category rather than anything particular to one firm. We checked the other lists ranking for the same queries: Thrive Internet Marketing Agency publishes a ten-agency ranking in which Thrive is first, scored 97 out of 100. SalesDuo publishes a twelve-agency comparison in which SalesDuo is first. Three lists, three publishers, three number ones.

What it should do is change how you read all of them, including this page. The rubric in a self-ranking list is chosen after the winner is known — pick retention rate and profit growth and one firm wins; pick channel breadth or contract flexibility and a different firm does.

So the same caution applies here, and we would rather state it than have you notice it: this page is published by a competing agency. Use it to know what to ask. Do not use it to make the decision, and do not use anyone else's list for that either.

A related transparency point we would rather raise than dodge. Neither Canopy nor Full Circle publishes prices for managed services. Darkroom does — a starting monthly figure for every service line on its own pricing page, Amazon management from $6,000/mo, in US dollars, read 20 August 2026. On that specific measure Darkroom is ahead of both of us.

The questions a review score cannot answer

A 4.9 tells you how thirty-five relationships went. It does not tell you how yours will be staffed or what happens if you want to leave. Those answers live in the contract and in the reference call, and neither shows up in a star rating.

Ask any agency on your shortlist — Canopy, us, anyone:

  • Who specifically runs this account day to day, how long have they been in that seat, and how many other accounts do they carry?
  • What is the initial term, does it renew automatically, and how much written notice is required — counted from what date?
  • Will you give written notice of any fee change, and how many days?
  • Who owns the ad account, the permissions, and any Amazon DSP advertiser seat used on our behalf?
  • What do we keep on exit — search-term history, audience definitions, reporting, any Amazon Marketing Cloud queries written for us?
  • What is inside the fee, and what has been billed on top of it for a client like us in the last year — photography, video, A+ content, marketplace expansion?
  • What happened the last time a client's performance dropped for a full month?

Then take two reference calls with brands of your size in your category, ask all seven again, and compare the answers rather than the decks. Agree offboarding terms at the start, while everyone is friendly and it takes five minutes.

The brief we would hand to Canopy, and the one we would keep

Being specific about this is more useful than a verdict, so here it is.

We would hand over: a brand with a healthy catalogue, reliable stock, compliant imagery and conversion at or above category norm, whose ceiling is genuinely media efficiency. That is an advertising problem, their reviewers say advertising is what they do best, and you should not pay a full-service team for scope you will not use.

We would keep: a brand where advertising is the symptom and the cause is sitting in the listings, the cases or the supply chain — where a search-term finding needs new creative, a fixed variation family and stock on the water before it can be acted on, and where those three things have to move together rather than in sequence across three vendors.

Which one you should actually pick

Canopy Management suits brands with a clean catalogue and reliable stock whose ceiling is media efficiency — their review record supports that specialism clearly and their own clients describe it. Full Circle suits brands where advertising is the symptom rather than the cause, and where listings, cases and supply have to move before spend can work. Buy the specialist when you have one problem, the generalist when you have four.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — on the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.

Common questions

Is Canopy Management a good Amazon agency?

On the public evidence, yes — 4.9 out of 5 across 35 Clutch reviews, with praise concentrated on advertising execution and communication rather than scattered across generic themes. Whether they are right for you depends on whether advertising is genuinely your constraint, because their own reviewers describe more limited operational and logistical support.

What do Canopy's critical reviews mention?

The criticism in their Clutch record is mild and specific: limited support in operational or logistical areas alongside the advertising work, requests for clearer dashboards and fewer acronyms in reporting, a wish for real-time progress tracking, and a small minority scoring cost lower than other categories. There is no pattern of service failure in the public record.

How much does Canopy Management cost?

They publish no prices on their own site. Their Clutch profile lists an hourly range of $150 to $199 and a $1,000+ minimum project size, but those are self-reported directory fields rather than a quote. Ask what the fee is, what makes it rise, whether photography and video sit inside it, and what the notice period is.

Should I trust an agency's own list of the best Amazon agencies?

Read it, then check where the publisher ranks itself. Canopy's guide puts Canopy first; Thrive's guide puts Thrive first; SalesDuo's puts SalesDuo first. The scoring criteria are chosen after the winner is known. The same caution applies to this page, which is published by a competing agency and says so.
