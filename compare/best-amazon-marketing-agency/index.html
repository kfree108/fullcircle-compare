Six ways to buy Amazon marketing

Agency shortlists usually compare logos. Compare structures instead — they survive staff turnover, and they predict behaviour.

  • Flat monthly retainer. Fixed fee, unaffected by spend or revenue. Budgeting is easy and scale is cheap. Nail down scope: the fee is identical in a busy month and a quiet one, so write down what is included and what is billed extra.
  • Percentage of ad spend. The fee tracks the ad budget, which keeps the agency close to the ad account and lets a small brand start cheaply. Agree upfront how the fee behaves as spend grows — caps and step-downs are normal and worth asking for.
  • Percentage of channel revenue. Ties the fee to total Amazon sales rather than the ad account. Define whether it is gross or net of returns, and what happens in a launch month.
  • Hybrid base plus performance. A base fee covering strategy, content and catalogue work, plus a component tied to results. The whole thing rests on the baseline — define it in writing before month one.
  • Productised single function. You buy one job at a published price rather than an agency relationship.
  • In-house plus software. Salaries and tooling instead of a fee. Total control, if you can hire and keep the person.

Who publishes, who quotes, and where we sit

Pricing in this category splits, and the split is worth getting right because most pages about it overstate one side. Some firms publish openly. Darkroom lists a starting monthly price for every service line on its own pricing page — Amazon management from $6,000/mo, TikTok Shop from $6,500/mo, paid media and retention marketing from $5,000/mo each, performance creative from $8,000/mo and retail media from $2,500/mo, all in US dollars, read on 20 August 2026. My Amazon Guy publishes its coaching rates plainly. On the specific measure of pricing transparency, both of them are ahead of us.

Others quote on a call, which is a normal commercial choice for scoped work rather than anything to hold against them. Canopy Management publishes no prices. Thrive describes "transparent, affordable pricing" and "no long-term contracts" on its Amazon page without stating a figure. Full Circle publishes no rate card for full-service management either, so on this measure we are in the quoting half of the market and Darkroom is not. We would rather say that than imply the whole category is closed.

My Amazon Guy also publishes a guide to account management cost stating that full-service management runs $3,000 to $12,000 per month, that flat retainers range from about $1,500 for basic offshore management to $25,000+ for enterprise teams, that percentage-of-ad-spend arrangements sit at 10% to 20%, and percentage-of-revenue arrangements at 3% to 8%. Read carefully: that page presents those as industry analysis, not as My Amazon Guy's own rate card. It never states what My Amazon Guy itself charges.

Third-party directories give you a second, weaker signal. Clutch lists My Amazon Guy and Thrive in a $100 to $149 hourly band and Canopy at $150 to $199, all with a $1,000+ minimum project size. Those are self-reported profile fields, not quotes.

Tie the model to the right measure

Whichever structure you pick, the number it reports against matters more than the number itself. Return on ad spend describes the ad account. Total advertising cost of sale describes the channel. An account can improve the first while the second gets worse.

Here is why the distinction is not academic. On Walkize, an account we can name publicly, the reported position was $267,131 in spend against $601,614 in attributed sales — 2.25x. Perfectly defensible on a monthly report. Underneath it, $89,885 of that spend, 33.6% of the total, had gone to search terms that produced no order at all, and the top 1% of terms were carrying 75.5% of all sales. A blended figure hides both facts completely.

One named account, not a claim about every client. But it sets the diligence question: ask what an agency reports monthly, and whether their fee moves with that same number. When the fee and the report point at different things, the report tends to win.

Where productised is the wrong answer: it does not chase Seller Support, argue a reinstatement, or own your catalogue. If those are your problem, buy a team.

Choosing the model that fits your account

Rules of thumb that hold up:

  • Spend is small, catalogue is clean. Productised or a percentage arrangement, not a large flat retainer.
  • Spend is large and growing fast. Flat retainer or a percentage with a cap, so the fee does not scale faster than the work.
  • Work is mostly catalogue, creative and cases. Flat retainer. Percentage arrangements track advertising, and advertising is not where the effort is going.
  • You already have an in-house operator. Buy Orbit or comparable software plus a specialist, not a full team.
  • You cannot describe your constraint in one sentence. Pay for a diagnostic first.

Which one you should actually pick

Flat retainers suit catalogue-heavy accounts. Percentage arrangements suit advertising-heavy ones, with a cap agreed upfront. Hybrids suit brands willing to define a baseline honestly. Productised services like Dr. PPC suit brands whose constraint is one function. In-house plus software suits brands who can hire and keep an operator. Match the structure to where the work actually is.

What to do with this

The right pick depends on how many hours a week the account will actually get. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. If nobody has four to ten hours a week to work that list, buy the work rather than the software.

Common questions

Which fee model is best for Amazon?

The one that matches where the work is. If most of the effort goes into catalogue, creative and cases, a flat retainer reflects reality better. If the effort is concentrated in the ad account, a percentage arrangement keeps the agency close to it. What matters more than the label is whether the fee and the monthly report point at the same outcome.

Do Amazon agencies publish their prices?

Some do. Darkroom publishes a starting monthly price for every service line, including Amazon management, and My Amazon Guy publishes its coaching rates. Many others quote on a call — Canopy Management, Thrive and Full Circle all publish nothing for full-service management, which puts us behind Darkroom on this particular measure. Where a listicle quotes a specific agency's monthly fee, check whether the number came from that agency's own page; usually it is a market range relabelled.

Should the fee be tied to ROAS?

Be careful with it. Return on ad spend can be improved by shrinking the ad account, which is not the same as growing the business. Total advertising cost of sale, contribution margin or total channel profit are harder to game and closer to what you are actually buying.

When is in-house cheaper than an agency?

Once the fee approaches a competent full-time salary and your catalogue is stable enough that one person can hold it. The failure mode is hiring one generalist to cover advertising, catalogue, creative and supply at once. If you go in-house, buy real software with them, or you have traded an agency for a spreadsheet.
