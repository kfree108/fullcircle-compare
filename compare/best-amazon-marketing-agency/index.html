Two different purchases wearing one word

On Amazon the word marketing is used for both halves of a business, and the halves behave nothing alike.

Advertising is the buying of placements: sponsored products, sponsored brands, sponsored display and programmatic display through Amazon's demand-side platform. It is measurable within days, adjustable within hours, and entirely contained inside one account.

Marketing is everything that determines what happens when a shopper arrives and what they are willing to pay. Images and video. Title and bullets. A+ content and the brand store. Price and the reference price behind it. Deals and coupons. Review volume and rating. Assortment and bundling. Off-Amazon demand — the social, email, creator and search traffic that arrives as branded search on Amazon and converts better than anything you can buy.

Almost every constraint that caps an ad account sits in the second list, which is why the distinction is not pedantry. You can improve a campaign structure for a quarter and never touch the thing that is actually limiting the account.

The practical version of this test: ask a candidate agency to name the last three changes they made for a client that were not in an ad account. If the answer is a struggle, you are buying advertising. That may be exactly right for you — but you should know which one you are buying, and it should be priced as what it is.

The demand you do not buy is the demand that pays best

The cheapest, best-converting traffic in an Amazon account is branded search, and you do not buy it in the ad account. It is manufactured everywhere else — by creators, by paid social, by email, by press, by a product people talk about.

That link is invisible until it breaks. On one account we watched total advertising cost of sale jump from 4% to 11% with no change whatsoever inside the ad account. The brand had cut its off-Amazon spend. Sponsored ads were suddenly buying demand that had previously been arriving free, and the deterioration looked exactly like ad decay. One account, one episode, told as a story rather than a rule — but the diagnostic takes ten minutes and it regularly saves a month of pointless bid work. Before optimising an efficiency drop, check what changed outside Amazon.

The mirror image is over-forecasting. Across our book, sending an email list to Amazon converts at around a quarter of a percent — roughly 500 orders per 200,000 emails — and weekend sends underperform. That is a portfolio aggregate across our managed accounts rather than a benchmark for yours. Brands consistently expect more because they benchmark against their own website, where the click lands on a page they control with the offer already applied. On Amazon the click lands in a marketplace with competitors adjacent. The channel is still worth running for velocity and rank, but it should be budgeted as a traffic tactic, not a revenue line.

An agency that only holds the ad account cannot see either effect and cannot act on it. That is not a criticism of advertising specialists — it is a scope fact, and the fix is to make sure somebody owns the other side.

Price and promotion: marketing levers with platform rules attached

Price is the most powerful marketing lever on Amazon and the one most often set without reference to how the platform treats it.

The rule that catches people: discount for more than about 45 days in any 90 and Amazon can treat the discounted price as your new reference price, which quietly disarms every promotion afterwards. Our working practice is to run headline deals for 14 days a month, often seven — long enough to earn the deal badge and the traffic that comes with it, short enough to protect the regular price the next promotion has to discount from. That is standing practice across our managed accounts. The planning consequence is that a promotional calendar is a budget of days, not a series of independent decisions.

The second thing worth doing is checking whether the promotion is doing anything at all. On one account a discount week produced $77,400 of revenue against $76,700 in a comparable week with no discount running. The traffic was already there; the promotion was not creating it. Nobody had checked, because a deal week always looks successful in isolation. That was one brand, told as a single observation — and on the other side of the same question, a separate account improved margin from 47% to 51% by raising price rather than discounting, validated by the brand's own finance team. Neither is a law. Both are cheap tests any seller can run before committing a year of promotional calendar.

Setting the price itself is a marketing decision that needs your website in the frame. The right Amazon price is set against your own delivered price including shipping, not your listed price. Worked in delivered terms, a channel-conflict argument usually turns into a two-line calculation.

Rating is an advertising cost before it is a conversion problem

Reviews get filed under brand or customer experience and then show up as an advertising bill.

When one product's rating slipped to 3.5 stars, its conversion rate barely moved. What halved was click-through rate — from 1.4% to 0.8% — and cost per click rose from $2.65 to $3.15. That was one account, a pet wellness brand, and it is an illustration rather than a benchmark. But the mechanism is worth understanding: the damage from a rating drop lands before the click, not after it. Shoppers who do click still buy at roughly the same rate. The ones who would have clicked are filtering you out in the search results, which raises your cost per click on an unchanged bid.

That reframes the response. The emergency is advertising efficiency, and the interim lever is widening match types toward cheaper traffic while the rating problem itself is worked at the product level.

It also sets a realistic timeline. The working ratio we use is seven five-star reviews to offset one one-star, and at low review counts eight to ten five-stars will move a 4.0 rating to roughly 4.2. Organic review rates run about 1 to 1.5% of orders, so that arithmetic sets a real calendar rather than a wish. We keep review-request velocity under 3% of trailing 30-day sales and only request on orders 30 to 60 days old, so the customer has actually used the product. That is standing practice across our managed accounts, and it is the compliant path — there is no legitimate shortcut, and removal of a bad review succeeds rarely enough that it should never be the plan.

If your Amazon problem is really a demand problem that starts elsewhere, that shape is right and an Amazon-only specialist is not. Their only published prices are coaching. Treating that guide as their rate card is a false statement about a company, and it appears in listicle after listicle.

Scope decides the fee model, not the other way round

Once you know whether you are buying marketing or advertising, the fee structure mostly follows.

  • Flat retainer fits marketing-heavy scopes, because the work is creative, catalogue and calendar rather than budget management. The fee is the same in a busy month and a quiet one, so write down what is included and what is billed extra.
  • Percentage of ad spend fits advertising-heavy scopes, because the fee tracks the thing being managed. Ask where it caps or steps down; both are normal to request.
  • Percentage of channel revenue ties the fee to total Amazon sales rather than the ad account. Define gross or net, and what happens in a launch month.
  • Base plus performance works only if the baseline is defined in writing before month one. Without that, the argument arrives in month nine.
  • Productised single function replaces the relationship with a defined output at a published price.
  • In-house plus software trades a fee for salaries and tooling, and works when you can hire and keep the operator.

Pricing in this category is genuinely split rather than uniformly closed. Darkroom publishes openly, My Amazon Guy publishes its coaching rates, and Canopy, Thrive and Full Circle all quote on a call. We would rather state that plainly than imply everyone hides.

What to do with this

The right pick depends on how many hours a week the account will actually get. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. If nobody has four to ten hours a week to work that list, buy the work rather than the software.

Common questions

What is the difference between an Amazon marketing agency and a PPC agency?

A PPC agency manages placements you buy. A marketing agency should also own what the shopper sees and pays: creative, price, promotion, reviews and the off-Amazon demand that feeds branded search. Many firms use the marketing label for the narrower job, so ask for the last three changes they made outside an ad account.

Do Amazon marketing agencies publish their prices?

Some do. Darkroom publishes a starting monthly price per service line, and My Amazon Guy publishes its coaching rates. Canopy, Thrive and Full Circle quote on a call. Where a roundup states a specific agency's monthly fee, check whether it came from that agency's own page — very often it is an industry range relabelled as a rate card.

Should the fee be tied to return on ad spend?

Be careful with it. Return on ad spend improves when you shrink the ad account, which is not the same as growing the business. Total advertising cost of sale, contribution margin or total channel profit are harder to game and closer to what you are actually buying. Whatever you pick, make sure the fee and the monthly report point at the same number.

Can marketing work fix a high advertising cost of sale?

Often it is the only thing that can. If click-through rate is falling because of rating or main image, or conversion is falling because of price or content, no bid change reaches the cause. Work out whether the loss is happening before the click or after it, then send the problem to whichever side of the account owns it.

How do I know whether my problem is demand or execution?

Compare a period when something changed outside Amazon with one when nothing did. If efficiency degraded while campaign settings held constant, look at off-Amazon spend, creator activity, seasonality and competitor entry before touching bids. Ten minutes of that check regularly saves a month of optimisation aimed at the wrong variable.

A real look at your account, not a pitch deck.
