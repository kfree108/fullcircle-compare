Amazon Agency Reviews: What Each Platform Is Actually Measuring

Updated 2026-08-20 · 2278 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon agency reviews”
The short answer

Amazon agency reviews live on four platforms that measure different things. Clutch entries are agency-nominated and analyst-verified, Trustpilot is open to anyone, G2 is built for software, and Google reviews mix clients with everyone else. The same firm routinely scores differently on two of them, and the gap is structural rather than suspicious.

The conflict, and why this page still works

Full Circle is an Amazon agency. Every firm named on this page is a competitor of ours, and we have no public review programme at all — no Clutch profile, no Trustpilot listing, nothing. That is a real gap in our own record and you are entitled to weigh it against us.

It also means we have no score to defend, which is the only reason a page like this can be written honestly. What follows is a method and a set of readings taken on 20 August 2026, all from profiles you can open yourself in a few minutes. Nothing here characterises any firm as good or bad. The argument is that the star average is the least informative field on any of these profiles, and that four other fields on the same page are worth more.

Four platforms, four different questions

Before you compare two numbers, know what produced them.

  • Clutch is nominated and verified. The agency puts a client forward, an analyst verifies or interviews them, and the published entry carries structured metadata: project size band, project length, service list and often a focus area. Nomination means the sample skews towards accounts the firm is proud of. This is not deception — it is how the platform is designed, and every listed agency is doing the same thing.
  • Trustpilot is open. Anyone can post without an invitation. The population that seeks out a review site unprompted skews to the two extremes, because middling experiences rarely generate the motivation to write. A firm can also invite reviews here, which changes the mix again.
  • G2 is built for software. Its question set assumes a product with features, onboarding and support. Applied to a service business, several of its categories do not map, and the corpus for agencies is usually thin.
  • Google reviews are unfiltered by relationship. Clients, candidates, former staff and people who once had a phone call all post in the same place, with no metadata to tell them apart.

Comparing a Clutch average with a Trustpilot average is therefore comparing two different populations answering two different prompts. It is not a fair fight in either direction, and any comparison article that puts them in the same column has already misled you.

What the divergence looks like in real numbers

Six Amazon-adjacent firms, both platforms where both exist, all read on 20 August 2026:

  • Seller Interactive — 4.2 across 47 reviews on Clutch; 3.5 across 27 on Trustpilot. Two substantial samples, disagreeing by seven tenths of a point.
  • Thrive Internet Marketing Agency — 4.6 across 109 on Clutch; 4.4 across 41 on Trustpilot. Two substantial samples, broadly agreeing.
  • AMZ Advisers — 4.4 across 30 on Trustpilot; two reviews on Clutch. We are not reprinting an average built on two entries in either direction, because two data points cannot characterise a firm that says it has worked with hundreds of brands.
  • SalesDuo — 4.8 across 42 on Trustpilot, of which 41 are five stars and one is four; their Clutch profile carries firmographics and no client reviews.
  • Emplicit — a Clutch profile with no review corpus attached and no Trustpilot record under either domain. The firm traded as Sunken Stone until it renamed in September 2022, so its older reviews sit under a name it no longer uses.
  • Full Circle — nothing on either platform.

Six firms, six completely different evidence situations. Any list that ranks these companies against each other on stars is ranking their review-collection programmes, not their work.

The four fields worth more than the average

Once you stop reading the score, the same pages become genuinely useful.

1. Sample size, and what it means for the firm's client type. A firm with two public reviews and a client list of large consumer-goods companies is not hiding anything — procurement departments do not post on directories. A firm with 109 reviews is running a collection programme well. Neither fact is about quality. Both are about who the clients are.

2. The distribution, not the mean. Forty-one five-star reviews and one four-star review is the ordinary signature of an invited programme: happy clients get asked, unhappy ones generally do not. A profile with genuine spread contains more information, because variance is the only thing you can learn from. Ratings cluster high across this entire category, which is exactly why they cannot separate anyone.

3. The project metadata. This is the field almost nobody reads and the one we would read first. Clutch publishes a project size band, a length and a service list on every entry. Read forty of those and you learn the firm's real operating range — which size of account it is built for, whether engagements run for years or for months, and which services actually generate the good reviews. A firm can be excellent at listing work and ordinary at advertising, and the blended average hides precisely that.

4. The text, and specifically its concreteness. A review describing purchase orders, shortage claims, a Vendor Central migration or a named account manager is evidence. A review saying a team was responsive and professional is a sentiment. Sort the corpus into those two buckets and the second bucket can be discarded without loss.

Two things that quietly invalidate a whole review corpus

The company changed its name. This category renames constantly and search engines keep the old names alive for years. Sunken Stone became Emplicit in September 2022. ChannelAdvisor became Rithum in December 2023 after being taken private. Acorn-i became Jellyfish Commerce. Flywheel Digital became a practice area inside Omnicom in January 2024, which is why its independent review corpus effectively stopped growing. Before reading any review, spend ninety seconds confirming the entity still trades under that name and has not changed hands.

The people changed. You are not hiring a company, you are hiring a pod: usually a strategist, an analyst who touches the account daily, and someone who answers the phone. A four-year-old five-star review is a review of people who have probably moved on. Weight the last twelve months heavily and everything older lightly, and ask directly how long your named analyst has been there and how many accounts they carry.

There is a third, softer factor worth naming: several vendors in the adjacent software space have repositioned so far that their reviews describe a product that no longer exists in that form. If a review does not mention the thing you are being sold, it is not evidence about the thing you are being sold.

What to do instead, in about three hours

Reviews narrow a list. They cannot pick from it. Replace the last step with evidence you generate:

  • Ask for two references in your band and category — not the firm's best account. One at your revenue, one that ended. How a firm handles the second request tells you more than either call.
  • Ask for one artefact instead of a proposal. A prioritised list of ten changes to your account with the evidence behind each and what would tell them it was wrong. Score on diagnosis, not presentation.
  • Ask who does the daily work and how many accounts they carry. A name and a number. This is the highest-information question in agency procurement.
  • Read the public replies to critical reviews. That is a live sample of how a dispute with you would be handled.
  • Get the boring clauses in writing. Notice period, written notice of any fee change, and what you keep on the day you leave: ad account ownership, bulk files, negative-keyword libraries, creative and dashboards. Ask us for all of it too.

Here is the kind of thing that first artefact should surface, from a named account we are cleared to discuss. On Walkize, $267,131 of ad spend produced $601,614 in attributed sales at 44.4% ACOS, and $89,885 — 33.6% of everything spent — went to search terms that produced zero orders, across 89,045 individual terms, while the top 1% of terms carried 75.5% of the sales. One account, one period, not a portfolio claim. A candidate who reads your data and does not find your version of that number has not read your data.

Which one you should actually pick

Use reviews to narrow a list, never to pick from it. Clutch tells you a firm's operating range through its project metadata; Trustpilot tells you what unprompted clients at the extremes felt; neither average is comparable to the other. Then generate your own evidence: two references including one that ended, one paid diagnostic artefact, and the exit clauses in writing — from us as readily as from anyone.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — on the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.

Common questions

Why does the same Amazon agency have different ratings on Clutch and Trustpilot?

Because the platforms sample different people. Clutch entries are nominated by the agency and verified by an analyst, which skews towards accounts the firm is proud of. Trustpilot is open to anyone, which skews towards the delighted and the aggrieved. A gap between the two is ordinary across the whole services category and is not evidence about any individual firm.

Are Amazon agency reviews trustworthy?

The individual accounts usually are. The averages are not comparable across platforms, and they cluster so high across the category that they cannot separate candidates. Read the text for concreteness, read the project metadata for operating range, weight the last twelve months heavily, and check whether the company has renamed or changed hands since the reviews were written.

What does it mean if an agency has very few reviews?

Usually that its clients are large. Procurement teams at consumer-goods companies do not post on directories, so firms serving them accumulate thin public records regardless of quality. It can also mean the firm renamed recently and the corpus sits under the old name. Neither is a warning sign; both mean you need references and a trial rather than a score.

Does Full Circle have reviews?

No. We have no Clutch profile, no Trustpilot listing and no invited review programme, which is a genuine gap in our public record. What we offer instead is two live references inside your revenue band and category — one of them an account that ended — plus a prioritised artefact built on your own ninety days of data before you commit to anything.

What should I ask a reference?

What changed in the first ninety days, who does the daily work and how many accounts they carry, what the agency got wrong and how it was handled, whether the fee has moved and with how much notice, and what they would buy differently if they were starting again. Then ask the same of a reference whose engagement ended.

A real look at your account, not a pitch deck.
