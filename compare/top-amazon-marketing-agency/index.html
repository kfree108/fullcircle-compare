Our position, up front

Full Circle is an Amazon management company, so we are one of the firms this page is telling you to interrogate. Interrogate us the same way.

We write about staffing rather than results because staffing is the variable a buyer can inspect before signing and results are the variable nobody can. Every agency in this category, ours included, can produce a case study that looks decisive, and almost none of them are lying. They are showing you their best account, selected after the fact, and it was usually staffed differently from the one you are about to be assigned.

What arrives is a pod and a share of its week

Whatever the deck says, the thing that shows up is a small group of people and a fraction of their attention. Four attributes decide what that group is worth to you.

  • Who sells and who executes. In most agencies the person in the room during the pitch is not the person opening your account on Tuesday. That is normal and not a criticism. It becomes a problem when the seniority gap is large and nobody mentions it.
  • Account load. The number of brands one manager carries is the most predictive figure in the whole exercise, and almost nobody volunteers it. A manager holding a handful behaves completely differently from one holding twenty, however capable they are.
  • Specialist depth against generalist coverage. A pod where one person handles advertising, listings, cases and stock coordinates faster and goes shallower on each. A pod with a specialist per function is the reverse. Both work; they suit different problems.
  • Cover. Who watches the account in the fourth quarter, over holidays, and while your manager is on leave. Ask for the named backup, not the reassurance.

None of those four appears in a case study, and all four are answerable in a sentence by any firm that has thought about them.

Account load, and what software actually changes about it

There is no industry standard for accounts per manager, and any agency quoting one as fact is improvising. What is reliable is the shape of the trade: fewer accounts means more attention and a higher fee, more accounts means a lower fee and heavier reliance on process and software. The honest question is not what the number is but what carries the load the number implies.

Here is our own arithmetic, offered because it explains the pricing of this whole category better than anything a competitor could tell you. The expensive part of ad management was never the thinking — it was the hours. In our own operation that meant nine specialists across 42 accounts, roughly 360 labour-hours a week. When we built an experiment that moved the repetitive execution into software, the entire model cost of running it came to roughly $100. That figure is our own product economics, reported internally rather than audited, and the point is not the hundred dollars. It is that the cost base of ad management changes shape when the mechanical sweep is automated, which is the actual reason software-managed advertising can be priced at hundreds a month rather than thousands.

What follows matters for how you read an account load. A manager holding twenty accounts with real tooling underneath them may give your account more useful attention than one holding six with a spreadsheet, because the parts that do not automate — judgement bids, quality assurance, and the conversation with you — are the parts you are actually paying for. So ask two questions rather than one: how many accounts, and what does the software do for that person before they open yours.

Automation is not the risk; unsupervised automation is

Every firm on your shortlist automates something. The question worth asking is who reviews the machine, how often, and against what reference.

We ask it because we got it wrong. A single bad line in an automation rule sheet on one account kept raising bids on campaigns whose cost of sale was already too high, and the tool did exactly what it was told for weeks. Nothing alerted, because a rule that is wrong still reports as running normally. That was one account, a supplement brand, and we are telling it as an incident rather than a pattern.

The correction was structural rather than tactical: one senior practitioner built a verified reference rule sheet, and every other account's rules are now cross-checked against it. That is the answer we would want a buyer to hear from anyone, including us.

The same question applies to written work. Generated drafts are a reasonable starting point and indefensible as a deliverable. Ask to see a redacted strategy document, then ask who read it before it went out and what they changed. A firm that cannot name the reader has told you how the document was made.

Rules-based bidding failing silently is the single strongest argument for a named human with time in their week. It is also the argument against paying for a large pod that spends its week doing what a machine should be doing.

Cover matters most in the weeks the intuition is wrong

Cover sounds like a hygiene question until a tentpole event lands and the account needs decisions at unusual hours.

Across 38 accounts we ran through one Prime event, 38% of revenue landed after 7pm, and the midnight-to-7am window outsold 1pm-to-4pm. The four-day event produced $4.55M across those accounts, and 29 of the 38 peaked on the Tuesday rather than on day one. That is a portfolio aggregate across our managed accounts for one event, reported internally, not a forecast for yours. The operational conclusion we draw from it is to stop dayparting during tentpole events, because the intuition about when shoppers are awake is built on ordinary weeks and is wrong for these ones. Afterwards, a 20 to 25% dip is normal and only worth investigating if it is steeper.

The staffing point underneath: a pod that is only present between nine and five in one time zone will make ordinary-week decisions during a week that is not ordinary. So ask specifically — who is watching this account during the event, what is the escalation path at 11pm, and what decisions is that person authorised to make without you.

Ask the same about the days after. Post-event is where budgets get cut on a dip that was always going to happen, and a team with the event in its memory will not make that call.

Reading review sites for staffing signals rather than scores

Review platforms are usually mined for the number. The staffing information hidden in the text is worth far more.

  • Do reviewers name their account manager? When a large share of a firm's reviews thank a specific individual, it usually means clients experience a stable relationship rather than a rotating queue. SalesDuo's public record reads that way — review after review is written about a named person rather than about the company, and several describe multi-year continuity on complex catalogues.
  • Do reviews mention handovers? Repeated references to a new account manager tell you something no star rating will.
  • Is the praise about responsiveness or about outcomes? Both are real and they describe different services. Responsiveness praise usually indicates good process; outcome praise usually indicates good analysis.
  • Date the review, then verify support on a reference call. Reviews for large firms routinely predate the current team, the current tooling and sometimes the current owner. A three-year-old review of a since-acquired agency is describing an organisation that may no longer exist in that form.
  • Employee-side sites are a turnover proxy, used gently. Read them for one thing only — whether a structural theme repeats across years — and ignore the score entirely.

Eight questions about the team, and what each answer reveals

  • Who exactly will work on this account, and what are their titles? Names, or it is not an answer.
  • How many other brands does that person carry, and what tooling do they have? The willingness to answer matters as much as the number.
  • How long have they been with you? Tenure predicts whether they have seen a fourth quarter, a suppression and a stockout.
  • Can I meet them before signing, without the salesperson in the room? Entirely reasonable, and revealing.
  • Who is their named backup, and when was cover last used?
  • Which parts of the work are automated, and who checks the machine against what reference?
  • What is your average client tenure, and how is it calculated? A retention claim with an undisclosed denominator is not yet a fact.
  • If my assigned lead leaves, what am I entitled to? Most contracts say nothing. Asking often gets a notice clause added, and the internal documentation question behind it — is the reasoning behind past decisions written down anywhere a successor can read — is the one that decides how bad the handover is.

Structures that change the maths, and who we would hand which brief to

Some firms have made structural choices that alter the equation rather than the effort.

  • Employee ownership. Tinuiti reports more than a thousand employee owners and $4B in digital media under management. Ownership guarantees nothing about continuity, but it changes the incentive to stay, and continuity is a large part of what you are buying.
  • Platform-native hiring. SalesDuo states that 85% of its team comes from an Amazon background. Claims of that kind are self-reported across this whole category, so ask how it is measured — but a deliberate hiring filter is a real design decision.
  • Creative capacity in-house. Darkroom builds around a design studio and short-form video production, which removes a queue that otherwise runs through a third party. They also publish a starting price per service line, so you can see what the creative line costs before the call. We publish nothing for full-service management, and that is a point to them.
  • No account manager at all. A productised service removes the pod from the equation and replaces it with a defined output. That is a genuine trade — you lose the person who notices things you did not ask about.

Which one you should actually pick

Judge an Amazon agency on the pod, not the brand. Ask for names, account load next to the tooling behind it, tenure, named cover for event weeks, who supervises the automation, and what happens on a handover. Large firms suit brands needing specialists per function; small ones suit brands needing a bigger share of a senior week; a productised service suits brands who want output and no relationship to manage.

What to do with this

The right pick depends on how many hours a week the account will actually get. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. If nobody has four to ten hours a week to work that list, buy the work rather than the software.

Common questions

How many Amazon accounts should one manager handle?

There is no standard, and a firm quoting one as fact is improvising. Judge the load against the tooling: a manager with real software carrying more accounts may give you more useful attention than one carrying fewer with a spreadsheet, because the automatable sweep is not where the value is. Ask for the number and for what the software does before they open your account.

Should I meet the team before signing with an Amazon agency?

Yes, and the request is normal. Ask for twenty minutes with the person who would run the account, without the salesperson present. You are checking two things: whether they can describe your category without being briefed, and whether they ask the questions a specialist would ask. Firms confident in their delivery team almost always agree.

Does agency size matter for Amazon?

It changes what you get rather than how good it is. Larger firms bring specialists per function, cover when someone is away, and platform relationships that help when something breaks. Smaller firms give you a bigger share of a senior person's week. The failure mode of the first is feeling like an account number; of the second, a single point of dependency.

What happens when my account manager leaves the agency?

In most contracts nothing formal — a replacement is assigned and you absorb the handover. It is the most common reason an engagement declines while nobody does anything wrong. Ask what internal documentation exists for your account, whether the reasoning behind past decisions is recorded where a successor can read it, and whether you get notice of team changes.

Is it a bad sign if an agency automates most of the work?

No. Unsupervised automation is the risk, not automation. A wrong rule keeps reporting as if it were running normally, which is why the useful question is who reviews the rules, how often, and against what verified reference. A firm that can describe that process has thought about the failure mode; a firm that treats the question as an accusation has not.
