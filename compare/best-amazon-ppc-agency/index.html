The twenty-minute diagnostic

Open Seller Central, go to the advertising reports section, and pull the search-term report for the longest window it will give you. Export it. You are after three figures, and a spreadsheet will produce all three in about twenty minutes.

  • Waste share. Sum the spend on every row where orders equal zero, then divide by total spend. This is the proportion of your budget that bought clicks and nothing else.
  • Losing-term count. Count those zero-order rows. Not the spend — the number of distinct terms. This tells you whether the waste is a handful of expensive mistakes or a long tail nobody has had time to read.
  • Concentration. Sort by sales, take the top one percent of terms, and work out what share of total sales they carry. This tells you how much of your revenue depends on a small set of queries.

For scale, here is what those three numbers looked like on Walkize, a brand that has cleared us to publish its account data. Between December 2025 and August 2026 the account spent $267,131 and produced $601,614 in attributed sales. Inside that, $89,885 — 33.6% of everything spent — went to search terms that produced no orders at all, spread across 89,045 individual terms, while the top 1% of terms carried 75.5% of the sales.

None of that is unusual. Long-tail waste at roughly a third of spend and revenue concentrated in a sliver of queries is close to the shape of a normal Amazon account. It is only shocking if nobody has ever shown you the arithmetic.

What your three numbers say about who to hire

The point of running the diagnostic is that different results point at genuinely different partners.

High waste share, very high losing-term count. Your problem is coverage and structure — nobody is reading the tail. This is the most common result, it is mechanical work, and it is the case where software or a productised service usually beats a retainer, because the job is a daily sweep rather than a strategy.

High waste share, low losing-term count. A few expensive targets are eating the budget. That is a judgement problem, not a volume problem, and a competent human on a small retainer will fix it faster than any tool.

Low waste share, extreme concentration. Your advertising is already efficient and your revenue rests on a handful of queries. A PPC agency has very little room left to help you. Your growth constraint is almost certainly elsewhere — conversion rate on the listing, catalogue coverage, review position, or stock. Hiring a bidding specialist here is the most expensive way to discover that.

Low waste share, low concentration, flat sales. You are running a broad, tidy account that has stopped growing. This is where advertising strategy — DSP, new-to-brand acquisition, AMC measurement — earns its fee, and where the big media agencies are genuinely strong.

Five questions that separate PPC agencies

  • Who or what actually changes the bids, and how often? There are three honest answers — a person, the agency's own software, or Amazon's built-in rules — and all three can work. What you want is a straight answer, because it tells you what you are paying for.
  • Show me what you would do in week one against these three numbers. Bring your diagnostic to the call. An agency that can talk specifically about your waste share and your concentration has read your account; one that responds with a capabilities deck has not.
  • What happens to the fee as spend rises? Ask for the fee at your current spend and again at double it. On a six-figure monthly budget, a single undisclosed percentage point is a five-figure annual difference, and it never appears in the headline number.
  • Who owns the data and the campaign structure if we stop? Campaigns live in your account, but bulk-sheet histories, negative libraries and dashboards sometimes do not travel.
  • Which of my numbers would make you say no to this account? The best answers to this question are the most specific ones.

Which one you should actually pick

Run the diagnostic before you shortlist. A long tail of losing terms points at a productised service or software with an operator behind it. A few expensive targets point at a small specialist retainer. Efficient advertising with stalled growth points away from PPC entirely, towards listings, catalogue or stock — Dr. Stock if the leak is stock. Buy the answer to your number, not the agency with the best deck.

What to do with this

The right pick depends on how many hours a week the account will actually get. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. If nobody has four to ten hours a week to work that list, buy the work rather than the software.

Common questions

How much of my ad spend should go to search terms with no orders?

There is no published benchmark worth trusting, and any agency that quotes you a universal target is guessing. What matters is the direction of travel in your own account, measured the same way each month. On Walkize the figure was 33.6% of spend across 89,045 terms before the work started — a normal shape for an account nobody had swept, and a number that only means something because it was measured consistently against itself.

Should I hire a PPC specialist or a full-service Amazon agency?

Run the diagnostic. If waste and structure are the story, a specialist is enough and cheaper. If your advertising is already efficient and growth has stalled anyway, the ceiling sits in conversion, catalogue or stock, and a bidding specialist cannot reach it. The mistake is expensive in one direction only: buying scope you never use costs money, but buying a specialist for a problem they cannot touch costs a year.

Do Amazon PPC agencies publish their prices?

Most do not — they quote on a call, which is normal for scoped services. Darkroom is a notable exception with a published starting price per service. My Amazon Guy publishes coaching rates plainly while quoting account management. We do not publish full-service pricing either; the one checkable number in our group is Dr. PPC at $300/mo plus 3% of ad spend, capped and month to month.

What is a fair Amazon PPC management fee?

Fair depends on the model, so compare the total at your spend rather than the headline. Ask each agency what you would pay this month, and what you would pay if spend doubled. Flat fees favour large spenders, percentage fees favour small ones, and a percentage without a cap is the arrangement that changes most as you grow. None of those structures is wrong; the undisclosed one is the problem.

Can software replace an Amazon PPC agency?

For the long-tail sweep, often yes — it is repetitive work that machines do without getting bored. For judgement calls about launches, competitive defence, catalogue decisions and what to do when a listing gets suppressed, not yet. The honest split is that software handles the volume and a human handles the exceptions, which is why the productised services in this category pair the two rather than choosing.
