What is published, and exactly where

These figures appear directly on My Amazon Guy's own coaching page, next to each coach, in US dollars, read on 20 August 2026. They are the only prices we could verify anywhere on their site.

  • 30-minute coaching calls: $150, $150, $200, $250 and $400, depending on which coach you book.
  • One hour with founder Steven Pope: $950.
  • Email a question: $50.

Verify those before you quote them anywhere, including back to us. Agency pages change, and a rate that was accurate last quarter is evidence about last quarter. A useful habit for any vendor page: note the date you read it, and check whether the page has a billing toggle you might be reading the wrong side of.

Publishing per-coach rates at all is more disclosure than most of this category offers. It is a small thing and it is worth crediting.

What is not published — stated neutrally, and completely

Full-service account management, advertising management, design, Vendor Central and Walmart work are all quote-only. The site routes to a fast-quote form, a free ASIN review and a booking link, with no rate card, tiers or minimums displayed.

That deserves to be stated without an accusation attached, and the rest of the picture stated accurately too, because it is frequently reported wrongly.

Most of this category quotes on a call. Canopy Management publishes no prices. Thrive publishes none on its Amazon page. Full Circle publishes none for full-service management either.

But it is not true that nobody publishes. Darkroom lists a starting monthly figure for every service line on its own pricing page — Amazon management from $6,000/mo, in US dollars, read 20 August 2026. On pricing transparency, Darkroom is ahead of My Amazon Guy and ahead of us. Saying so costs us nothing except the pleasure of a one-sided argument.

And within the half of the market that quotes, My Amazon Guy sits at the transparent end. Publishing coaching rates, and publishing a detailed public analysis of what agency work costs, is more disclosure than most firms that quote bother with. Whatever you conclude about fit, that is a fair reading of what is actually on the site.

One more variety of published-but-different that is worth knowing: Thrive states on its Amazon page that its Amazon marketing services come with "no long-term commitments". That is not a price, but on a twelve-month view it can matter more than one.

The ranges on their blog are not their prices

This is where most articles about this keyword go wrong, so read it slowly.

My Amazon Guy publishes a guide to Amazon account management cost containing real, specific numbers: full-service account management at $3,000 to $12,000 per month, growing brands landing at $3,500 to $6,000 and established seven-figure brands nearer $6,000 to $12,000; flat retainers from about $1,500 for basic offshore management up to $25,000+ for enterprise teams; percentage-of-ad-spend arrangements at 10% to 20%; and percentage-of-revenue arrangements at 3% to 8%.

Every one of those is presented on the page as analysis of what agencies in general charge. The framing sentence opens with what full-service account management costs across the market, and the page cites independent analyses as its support. It never states what My Amazon Guy itself charges, and the calls to action route to a quote.

So anyone telling you "My Amazon Guy costs $3,000 to $12,000 a month" has taken a market range off a blog post and relabelled it as a rate card. That is a false statement about a company, and it is in circulation widely.

The test, which we apply to ourselves after getting it wrong before: does the sentence around the number say "agencies charge" or "we charge"? If it is the first, the number is market commentary and belongs in a paragraph about the market, never in a column headed with a firm's name. Several agencies publish industry cost guides that look exactly like pricing pages, and this is the single most common factual error in agency-comparison content.

Used correctly, the guide is genuinely useful. It is a reasonable public benchmark for what this work costs, published by someone with a large enough book to know. It is simply not a quote.

The one third-party signal worth noting: their Clutch profile lists a $1,000+ minimum project size, a $100 to $149 hourly band, an employee count of 250 to 999 and a Duluth, Georgia headquarters. Those are self-reported directory fields, not proposals.

Read the contract, not the quote

Two proposals with identical monthly figures can differ by a factor of two over three years, and the difference is entirely in the paperwork that nobody in this genre writes about.

To show why it matters, a real published example from an adjacent vendor, cited neutrally because a real document is easier to reason about than a hypothetical. Quartile's terms and conditions, read 20 August 2026, set an initial term of "one year from the Effective Date", automatic renewal "for successive one-year periods", non-renewal only "by providing written notice of non-renewal at least sixty (60) days prior to the expiration", fees "due in advance of Services rendered", and no entitlement "to any refund in connection with any termination". Month-to-month exists only as a written exception the vendor may grant.

None of that is improper. It is an entirely ordinary enterprise agreement. The point is that it is published, decision-relevant, and quoted by nobody — every article comparing this category compares services while the renewal window quietly does the deciding. Miss the notice date and you have bought another year.

So ask every agency, in writing, before the fee conversation:

  • What is the initial term, and does it renew automatically?
  • How many days' written notice, counted from what date?
  • Are fees payable in advance, and is any part refundable if we leave mid-term?
  • Will you give written notice of any fee change, and how many days?
  • Who owns the ad account, the permissions and any Amazon DSP advertiser seat used for us?
  • What do we keep on exit — search-term history, reporting, audience definitions, any Amazon Marketing Cloud queries?
  • What happens to our rate and our team if the agency changes ownership?

That last one is not paranoia. This category has reorganised heavily — Sunken Stone relaunched as Emplicit, ChannelAdvisor became Rithum, Carbon6 was acquired by SPS Commerce. Several of those firms are stronger for it, and none of this is a warning about any of them. It simply makes change-of-control and price-protection language a reasonable ask of anyone, us included.

Make the quotes comparable before you compare them

Since most of your shortlist will quote rather than publish, the skill is normalisation. Ask all of these, of every agency, in writing, and put the answers in one table:

  • What is in the base fee, itemised — advertising, listings, catalogue work, case management, creative, reporting, meetings.
  • What triggers an increase — ad spend thresholds, revenue thresholds, ASIN count, marketplace count, seat count.
  • What is billed on top. Photography, video, A+ content, translations and marketplace launches are the usual separate lines.
  • Onboarding and setup fees — one-time, or repeating on scope change?
  • Notice period and offboarding — how much notice, and what happens to permissions and the ad account the day after.
  • Who owns the data — do you keep historical reporting when you leave?
  • Does the software cost extra, and is it theirs or licensed from someone else?
  • Who is actually assigned, and how many other accounts do they carry?

Then do the arithmetic yourself rather than accepting the summary. Multiply the monthly by twelve, add every line that sits outside the base fee, and add the loaded cost of your own people who will be servicing the relationship. Do that on every vendor including us — annual figures and monthly figures on the same page have caught us out before, and checking is a two-minute job that nobody regrets.

Pay particular attention to any percentage attached to ad spend. It is the half of the deal that grows, and it grows fastest in the months you are trying to scale. At $100,000 a month in media, a single undisclosed percentage point is $12,000 a year — bigger than most of the differences people spend weeks negotiating.

Which pricing model actually suits you

The structure matters more than the number, because the structure decides the incentive.

  • Flat retainer. Predictable and easy to budget. Best when your spend is large relative to your revenue, or when the work is operational rather than media-driven. The risk is a fixed fee against a fast-growing account quietly getting under-serviced.
  • Percentage of ad spend. Ties the fee to the account rather than to hours. Best when advertising genuinely is the growth lever. It needs two safeguards in writing: a rate that steps down as spend rises, and a review trigger if spend grows faster than results. Ask for a cap.
  • Percentage of revenue. Aligns to the outcome you actually care about, and is the hardest to attribute fairly, since the agency gets paid for organic revenue it did not touch. Define the baseline before you sign.
  • Hourly or project. Right for a defined job — a reinstatement, a catalogue rebuild, a launch — and wrong as a way to run an account long-term.
  • Productised, published price. Right when the scope is genuinely fixed and you want a number without a sales call.

There is no honest universal answer. We charge a percentage on some services and a published flat-plus-percentage on others, so we are not going to attack any of these models — the fair criticism of ours is that a percentage grows with your spend, and the fair defence is the cap and the disclosure, not an adjective.

What we publish, and what we don't

Full Circle is a full-service Amazon management company with $500M+ in managed spend across 100+ brands, including publicly named work with HexClad, Ridge, BK Beauty, Beardbrand, Epic Gardening and The Woobles.

We do not publish a rate card for full-service management. It is quoted per account, and by the standard set earlier on this page, that is a fair thing to hold against us. Darkroom does it better.

What we do publish is the price of our productised services, because owning our own software rather than renting someone else's is what makes a fixed price possible in the first place. Dr. PPC is advertising management on its own at $300/month plus 3% of ad spend, capped, month-to-month, with the first 30 days free and Orbit — the software suite — included at no additional charge. Take that route if you want a number you can read before a sales call.

On value rather than price: what you are actually buying is the ability to act on what the data says. On Walkize, one named account, $267,131 of spend produced $601,614 in attributed sales — 2.25x at 44.4% ACOS — while $89,885, 33.6% of everything spent, went to search terms that produced no orders at all, and the top 1% of terms drove 75.5% of all sales. One account, not a promise about yours.

But the test generalises, and it is the most useful thing on this page. Whichever agency you pick, ask them what percentage of your spend currently returns nothing. Any agency with report access can answer that in an afternoon. The quality and speed of that answer predicts the engagement far better than the fee does.

What to do with this

Before you compare subscription prices, pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders. On the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Whatever you buy — a seat, a service, or nothing — that number is the one it has to move, and a cheaper tool nobody has time to drive will not move it.

Common questions

How much does My Amazon Guy charge for account management?

They do not publish it. The site routes to a quote form for full-service management, advertising, design, Vendor Central and Walmart work. The only prices published plainly are coaching rates. Any specific monthly figure attributed to them elsewhere is very likely a market range lifted from their own industry cost guide and relabelled.

Is the range in their cost guide My Amazon Guy's own price?

No. Those figures appear in their published guide to Amazon account management cost, framed as analysis of what agencies charge in general and supported by citations to independent analyses. The page never discloses what My Amazon Guy itself charges. It is a useful market benchmark and a poor substitute for a quote.

What are the published My Amazon Guy coaching prices?

On their coaching page, read 20 August 2026 in US dollars: 30-minute calls at $150, $200, $250 and $400 depending on the coach, an hour with founder Steven Pope at $950, and $50 to email a question. Check the page directly before relying on any of them, since rates change.

Do any Amazon agencies publish full pricing?

Some do. Darkroom publishes a starting monthly price for every service line, including Amazon management. Many others quote instead — Canopy Management, Thrive and Full Circle publish nothing for managed services, which is a point against us and in Darkroom's favour. Productised services are the other place published numbers exist, because a fixed scope permits a fixed price.

What should I compare besides the monthly fee?

What is itemised in the base fee, what triggers an increase, what is billed on top, onboarding fees, the notice period and offboarding terms, who owns your historical data and any DSP seat, whether software is included, and who is actually assigned to the account. Two quotes at the same monthly number can differ enormously once those are answered.
