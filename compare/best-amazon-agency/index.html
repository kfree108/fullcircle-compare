This is a procurement exercise, and almost nobody runs it as one

The usual method is to gather names from a ranked list, take three sales calls, feel better about one of them, and sign. That is not an evaluation. It is a personality test with a two-year contract attached, and it explains why so many brands are on their third agency in four years while believing each individual decision was reasonable.

An Amazon agency is typically a six-figure annual commitment with privileged access to your ad account, your catalogue and your commercial data. Companies run harder processes than this for a warehouse lease. The good news is that a rigorous version costs about three weeks and produces a decision you can defend internally afterwards, which matters more than it sounds when the first bad month arrives.

Four stages, in order, and the order is the point:

  • A brief every candidate receives identically, so you are comparing answers rather than access.
  • A live account read, timed, with the same permissions granted to each.
  • Seven questions put to all three, with the answers written down.
  • Four contract clauses settled before anybody quotes a price.

Price last is deliberate. A number is meaningless until you know what sits inside it, and quoting first anchors the whole conversation on the cheapest scope rather than the right one.

Stage one: write a brief that can actually be answered

Most agency briefs describe the company and ask for a proposal. That produces three decks about the agency. What you want is three answers to the same question, which means the question has to be specific enough to be answerable and identical for everyone.

A workable brief fits on two pages and contains five things.

  • Your last twelve months at channel level: revenue, ad spend, total advertising cost of sale, and the direction each has moved.
  • The number you want changed and by when. Not growth in general. Contribution margin, non-branded revenue, unit velocity on a named product line — one of them, with a date.
  • Your constraints. Stock lead times, margin floor, categories you will not discount, channel-conflict rules with your own site.
  • What is already covered internally, so nobody prices work you do not want.
  • How you will decide, including this list of stages. Telling candidates the process is not a weakness; it filters out the ones who only perform well in an unstructured conversation.

Give every firm the same document on the same day, with the same read-only access, and say so. An agency working from a fuller export will look sharper for reasons that have nothing to do with capability, and you will not be able to tell the difference afterwards.

Stage two: fifteen minutes inside your live account

This is the single most discriminating thing in the whole process and it costs you nothing. Grant each finalist read-only access, give them fifteen minutes on a call, and ask one question: what is the first thing you would change, and what number would tell you it worked?

The split is immediate. Some candidates describe a methodology. Some open a report and find something. There is no third group.

What a real finding looks like, from an account we can name: Walkize arrived with $267,131 of Amazon ad spend against $601,614 in attributed sales, a 2.25x return. Nothing about that headline is alarming, and two agencies could argue about it indefinitely. The search-term data was not arguable. $89,885 of that spend — 33.6% of everything they had spent — had gone to terms that produced no order at all, spread across 89,045 individual search terms, while the top 1% of terms carried 75.5% of all sales. That sits inside 30,921 unique terms and 244,510 clicks at a $1.09 blended cost-per-click. One named account, from our own data, not a typical result and not a promise about yours.

The point is the resolution, not the size. A finding that specific comes from opening the account rather than from a framework.

A second question worth asking in the same fifteen minutes: how many campaigns in this account actually spent money last month? On one enterprise vendor account we audited, roughly 17,000 campaigns existed and fewer than 200 had spent anything in the previous 60 days. Nothing was broken — every one of them was technically active, accumulated through agency changes, tool migrations and seasonal builds nobody archived. That was one account, told as a story. But the number of campaigns that actually spend is the real size of an account, and a candidate who reaches for it unprompted is telling you how they work.

Stage three: seven questions, asked of everyone

Ask all three the same seven and write the answers down verbatim. Comparing remembered impressions a fortnight later is how the most charming presenter wins.

  • Who does the daily work, and how many other brands do they hold? The person on the sales call is rarely the person in the account on Tuesday. The willingness to give a number matters as much as the number.
  • What would you look at first, and what would tell you it worked? A good answer names a report and a metric. A weaker one names a process.
  • Which of catalogue, cases, creative and inventory do you actually touch? Both answers are legitimate; only one matches what is capping you.
  • What software am I on, do I keep access if I leave, and is it billed separately? Agencies renting their tooling pass both the cost and the limits through to you.
  • How long do you leave a change before judging it? Our own working window is four to eight weeks, and an account takes three to four weeks to settle after a spend restoration — that is standing practice across our managed accounts. Short windows read ordinary variance as signal, which produces a cut, which produces a real decline, which reads as confirmation. A candidate who promises to prove themselves in ten days is promising to misread your data.
  • What is billed on top of the fee? Photography, video, case work and clean-room analysis are frequently separate lines.
  • Tell me about an account you lost, and what you would do differently. The one question with no rehearsed answer.

On the last of those, here is the standard we would want held to us. We once ran a forensic audit on a declining account and concluded, on the record, that we had caused the decline: campaign consolidation and spend cuts made chasing a conservative cost-of-sale target had killed the account's best-performing campaigns. The fix was ordered the same day — restore spend, accept a worse efficiency ratio for a period, expect three to four weeks before anything moved. That was one account, a beauty and wellness brand, and it recovered to record weekly revenue in about five weeks. Listen for whether a candidate can audit their own changes before they audit the market.

Stage four: four clauses, settled before price

The commercial terms outlast every person who negotiated them. These four decide more about the next two years than the monthly number does.

The fee basis, in words. If any part of the fee is a percentage, say precisely what it is a percentage of. One client — a personal-care brand — challenged being charged on gross Amazon revenue rather than net, and they were right: gross includes refunds and returns, so the brand was paying a percentage on its own returns. It is a small clause with a large compounding effect on a scaling account. We amended our contracts to a net basis, and it is a fair question to put to any agency including us.

Written notice of fee changes. Ask for it, from any agency including us. Rates move, scopes drift, and the difference between a conversation and a surprise is a clause.

Team change. Most contracts say nothing about who is assigned, which is why the most common quiet decline in an engagement follows a handover nobody was told about. Ask for notice of a change to your named lead, and ask what internal documentation exists so a successor can read the reasoning behind past decisions rather than starting from the dashboard.

Exit. Notice period, who holds the ad account and brand store, what you receive on the way out, and how fast access transfers. Settle it while everyone is friendly and it costs five minutes.

If Amazon is one channel of five, they are the sensible pick and we are not.

What to do with this

The right pick depends on how many hours a week the account will actually get. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. If nobody has four to ten hours a week to work that list, buy the work rather than the software.

Common questions

How many Amazon agencies should I shortlist?

Three. Two gives you no spread to judge against and five turns into a quarter-long procurement exercise. Give all three the same brief, the same access and the same fifteen minutes, then ask each for the single biggest thing they would change first. The disagreements between them teach you more about your own account than the agreements do.

Should I trust ranked lists of the best Amazon agencies?

Use them to collect names, never to pick. Most are published by agencies that appear in their own ranking, by directories that sell placement, or by publishers earning a referral. This page is published by an agency too. Take the names into the process above and let the live account read decide.

How much should I expect to pay an Amazon agency?

Very few publish a rate card, including us. The structures you will meet are a flat retainer, a percentage of ad spend, a percentage of channel revenue, or a base fee with a performance component. Ask what the fee is, what makes it rise, what it is calculated on, and what is billed separately.

What is the single most revealing question to ask an agency?

Tell me about an account you lost, and what you would do differently. It is the one question in a sales process with no rehearsed answer. Listen for whether they audit their own decisions against the timeline or attribute the loss to the client, the category or the platform.

Can I run a paid trial instead of a full evaluation?

A paid diagnostic month is a reasonable middle path, provided you define the deliverable before it starts and keep your data on your side of the line. What it will not tell you is how the relationship behaves under pressure, which is what the contract clauses are for. Run the trial and settle the clauses anyway.
