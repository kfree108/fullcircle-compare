Disclosure first, then the numbers

Full Circle competes with SalesDuo for Amazon management work. We sell against them and we lose briefs to them. Read this the way you would read any competitor's assessment of a rival — which is to say, check it. Every figure below comes from a public profile you can open, pulled on 21 August 2026 from the platform that issues it rather than from any site that aggregates ratings.

  • Trustpilot — TrustScore 4.8 across 43 published reviews, spanning 2024 to the present. All but one of the visible entries are five stars.
  • Clutch — a profile exists, listing the firm as founded in 2018 with 50 to 249 employees and a Seattle base. It carries no client reviews, and both its minimum project size and its hourly rate are recorded as undisclosed.

Their own site lists offices in New York, Newark, Seattle, Dubai and Bengaluru. It publishes no price anywhere, which is the norm in this category and is worth stating neutrally rather than as an accusation — we publish no full-service pricing either.

One thing to note about method before anything else. All the major review platforms refuse ordinary automated requests, which is why so many articles about agencies quote a rating from a search snippet or an aggregator. We do not do that any more, because we got it badly wrong once doing exactly that. Every score on this page is printed with its count and its platform, because a score without its count is not a fact.

Why the score is the least useful part of that record

A distribution of 43 reviews with essentially all of them at five stars is not evidence of perfection, and nobody should read it as such — including SalesDuo, who have not claimed it. It is the ordinary signature of an invited review programme: happy clients get asked, unhappy ones generally do not, and most reviews arrive through a link rather than through a verified transaction.

That practice is standard across the whole services category and it is not manipulation. It simply means the average tells you about the collection method, and the text tells you about the firm. Nearly every agency you compare will carry a high average for exactly the same reason, which is precisely why the average cannot separate them from each other.

The count is more informative than the score, and here it says something specific. Forty-three reviews on one platform and none on another is a firm that has concentrated its review-solicitation effort in one place rather than a firm without clients — the empty Clutch profile is not a verdict and should not be read as one. What it does mean is that you have a single collection channel to reason about, so the usual cross-check of comparing two independent populations is not available to you.

So read the text. On this profile it is unusually informative, because reviewers describe concrete operational work rather than generic satisfaction, and that content is checkable in a way a star is not.

What reviewers consistently describe

  • Vendor Central and 1P mechanics. Several reviews describe EDI work — purchase orders, advance ship notices, invoices — plus shortage claims and chargeback disputes pursued through to recovery. This is unglamorous, genuinely difficult work that most Amazon agencies do not take on at all, and it is the clearest specialism in the record.
  • Catalogue depth. One long-standing client describes running more than 50,000 ASINs and credits the setup, data management and ongoing optimisation across that catalogue over five years. Large catalogues break most workflows; a five-year account at that size is a real signal rather than a testimonial.
  • Platform migrations. A reviewer describes moving an entire catalogue from Vendor Central to Seller Central with SalesDuo running it, which is among the higher-risk projects a brand can undertake on Amazon.
  • Multi-marketplace operations. Reviews describe Amazon and Walmart 3P accounts managed together rather than Amazon alone.
  • Named account managers. A striking share of reviews are written about a specific person by name rather than about the company. That pattern usually indicates stable, senior relationships rather than a rotating support queue.
  • Cadence. Weekly meetings and responsiveness recur throughout — and being visibly present is the thing brands most often say is missing at other agencies.

The one criticism in the whole record, and the clause it points at

Across the visible reviews on the day we read the profile there was exactly one that is not five stars, and its substance is worth setting out because it is the only critical data point available. The reviewer describes a positive engagement covering listings, creative and marketing, then notes that a price increase was not communicated in advance, which came as a surprise and did not feel transparent for a relationship built on trust.

That is a mild, specific and entirely believable complaint, and it is more useful than any amount of praise, because it points at something you can settle contractually before it happens rather than argue about afterwards.

  • Ask what has triggered a fee change for existing clients in the past twelve months.
  • Ask for written notice of fee changes, from any agency including us — sixty or ninety days is a normal request and most firms will agree to it.
  • Ask whether the fee is indexed to anything: ad spend, revenue, ASIN count, or the number of marketplaces.
  • Ask which revenue the fee is measured against, and whether it is gross or net of refunds and returns. A percentage taken on gross charges a brand for its own returns, and the difference compounds on a scaling account.
  • Get every answer into the contract rather than the email.

Apply all of that to every agency on your list, ourselves included. Undisclosed fee movement is a category-wide risk rather than a SalesDuo trait, and the only reason it surfaces here at all is that one of their own reviewers was candid about it. A firm whose sole public criticism is a communication failure on a price rise is, on the evidence available, in reasonable shape.

What operational Amazon work actually looks like from inside

Reviews describing appeals, chargebacks and reinstatements tell you the work happened. They do not tell you what makes it hard, and that gap is where most brands mis-scope an agency relationship. Two things from our own practice, offered so you can ask better questions of whoever you hire.

Replying to your own appeal to chase it can restart the review from the beginning. Amazon support confirmed that to us privately and it is not documented anywhere a seller would look. The practices that follow are specific: keep exactly one case identifier, request an internal review rather than filing a fresh appeal, call every three days and have the representative document the call inside the case, and never send a reminder email into an open appeal. One appeal in our book ran five weeks without any response at all — not even a rejection — before parallel escalation tracks moved it. That is practice developed across multiple client appeals rather than a published rule, and it is the kind of thing that separates an operational team from a hopeful one.

The second is about what those weeks feel like from the client's side. Across our own client base, the most common reason a brand loses confidence in an agency is not bad results — it is a false impression of inactivity while the work happens out of sight. Compliance escalations, catalogue repairs and forecasting produce no visible artefact for weeks. Our internal shorthand is that it is the surprises and the silence that anger people, not the problems themselves, and the answer is unromantic: report the invisible work explicitly, and report the bad weeks first.

Which turns into a question worth asking SalesDuo, us, or anyone else: when a case is open and nothing has moved for three weeks, what will I receive, and how often? The reviewers on that profile who praise weekly cadence are, whether they frame it that way or not, describing a firm that answered that question well.

Their published claims, and how to check them — ours included

SalesDuo's own site carries several headline figures: 300+ brands, $3B revenue managed, 85% of the team from an Amazon background, an average NPS of 9.1 and 80% average year-over-year growth for clients. Their programmes are named Starter, Growth and Scale, banded by client revenue — Growth for brands between $100K and $5M, Scale above $5M — with no price attached to any of them.

Those are self-reported and unaudited, as agency figures generally are across this category, and we are not suggesting otherwise about them specifically. The questions worth asking about any of them are always the same three: what is the denominator, over what period, and does the average include the accounts that left?

So here are ours with the denominator attached. Full Circle is a full-service Amazon management company with $500M+ in managed spend across 100+ brands, including publicly named work with HexClad, Ridge, BK Beauty, Beardbrand, Epic Gardening and The Woobles. And rather than an average, one named account with the whole dataset behind it: on Walkize, $267,131 of ad spend produced $601,614 in attributed sales, while $89,885 — 33.6% of spend — went to search terms that returned no orders at all and the top 1% of terms carried 75.5% of the sales. That is one account over one period, read out of our own database, and we are not presenting it as a portfolio result.

One place they are ahead of us, plainly. SalesDuo has a substantial public review record and we run no public review programme at all. If third-party verification matters to you, that is a real point in their favour and there is nothing for us to point at in reply.

Who they suit better than we do

If your Amazon problem is 1P — Vendor Central purchase orders, EDI, shortage claims, chargebacks accumulating faster than anyone can dispute them — SalesDuo is a serious answer and their review record supports that specialism in reviewers' own words rather than in marketing copy. The same holds if you have a very large catalogue that needs disciplined data management, or if you are contemplating a move between Vendor Central and Seller Central and want a team that has run one before. Their marketplace and geographic breadth is also wider than ours.

We would keep the brief where advertising, listings, catalogue and supply have to move together, and where acting on a finding in one requires authority over the other three. And where owning the software matters, because you want the account data acted on rather than reported: Orbit is ours, built in-house, and it is included with every product in the group rather than sold as an extra subscription.

If you would rather buy one function than an agency, three honest redirects. Dr. Stock covers inventory, fees and supply chain as a product — use it when the money is leaking through stock and fees rather than media, which is more often than brands expect. Dr. PPC runs the ad account at a published $300/month plus 3% of ad spend, capped, month to month, with the first 30 days free — use it when advertising is the only function you want handled and you do not want an account team to manage. And if display and video are the growth question rather than search, Dr. DSP is that as a product and reMKTR is the same capability bought as a service on our seats. All four came out of Full Circle, which remains the answer when what you need is the whole team rather than one function.

Which one you should actually pick

SalesDuo suits brands whose Amazon difficulty is operational — Vendor Central, EDI, chargebacks, enormous catalogues, or a migration between 1P and 3P — and the review record supports that specialism in the reviewers' own words. They are also ahead of us on third-party verification, since we run no public review programme at all. Full Circle suits Amazon-first brands where advertising, listings, catalogue and supply have to move together. Read either record for what it describes rather than for the star rating, and check the count before the score.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — on the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.
