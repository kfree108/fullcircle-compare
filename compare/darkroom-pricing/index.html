Darkroom pricing: what they publish, and what a starting figure leaves open

Updated 2026-08-21 · 2972 words · Written against what currently ranked for “darkroom pricing”
The short answer

Darkroom publishes a plain rate card: a starting price for every service line, including Amazon management, on their own pricing page. That is rare in this category and worth crediting. What the page does not state is the scope at that starting tier, or whether any percentage of media sits on top. Both are questions for the call.

All fourteen lines, read on 21 August 2026

Their pricing page carries a starting figure against every service line. Eleven are marked per month: Amazon management $6,000/mo, TikTok Shop management $6,500/mo, organic social management $6,000/mo, paid media management $5,000/mo, retention marketing $5,000/mo, creator content $5,000/mo, performance creative $8,000/mo, growth strategy $10,000/mo, conversion rate optimisation $5,250/mo, AI search $4,500/mo, and retail media management $2,500/mo.

Three carry no monthly marker at all: brand studio from $12,500, website development from $60,000, and website support from $3,500 — the last of which Darkroom itself describes as a senior Shopify team on retainer. Check which basis applies to the line you want before you compare it with anything, because a project figure and a monthly figure set side by side in the same list is the easiest misreading available on that page.

The eleven monthly lines are starts-at figures in US dollars, and they are the floor of a scoped quote rather than a fixed price for a defined package. Read the page yourself before you rely on any of them; rate cards move, and a figure copied out of a comparison article six months later is worth nothing.

One thing to separate carefully. Darkroom also publishes a long article about what marketing agencies cost in general. That piece is market analysis, and the ranges in it describe the industry rather than what Darkroom charges. Relabelling somebody's industry guide as their rate card is a false statement about a company, and it happens constantly in this category — My Amazon Guy's published $3,000 to $12,000 range and 10% to 20% of spend are the best-known example, and they are explicitly industry analysis. Their only published prices are for coaching. Darkroom's own prices are the ones on the pricing page and nowhere else.

Credit where it is due, and what it costs them to give it

We compete with Darkroom, and this is still the honest assessment: publishing a starting price for each service is the single most useful thing an agency can do for a buyer, and the category overwhelmingly does not do it.

Tinuiti publishes no rate and quotes on a call. Canopy Management and Thrive publish nothing either. My Amazon Guy publishes coaching prices plainly and quotes account management. And we should be exact about ourselves before pointing anywhere: Full Circle publishes no full-service pricing at all. It is scoped on a call, like most of this market, which puts us behind Darkroom on this specific measure and there is no way to dress that up.

It is worth understanding what publishing costs a firm, because it explains why so few do it and it is not cowardice. A published floor loses you every enquiry from a brand below it — including brands that would have been talked up into a bigger scope on a call. It also anchors every negotiation you do have at the number on the page. A firm that publishes is trading pipeline volume for buyer trust and for time saved on unqualified calls. That is a legitimate strategy rather than a moral position, and the same is true of quoting.

You can see who the card is written for elsewhere on their own site: the lowest band on Darkroom's enquiry form for estimated media budget starts under $1 million. If you are materially smaller than the buyer that page is written for, a starting figure will still tell you what you need in about ten seconds, which is more than a quote-only competitor will do for you at all.

The trade this category never names: prices or reviews, rarely both

Here is something that only became visible when we started opening the primary records rather than reading roundups. We pulled every public review profile for the firms on this page on 21 August 2026, from the platforms that issue them rather than from any aggregator.

Darkroom's Clutch profile carries no client reviews. It records the firm as founded in 2017, 50 to 249 employees, with a minimum project size of $5,000+ — which is a second published price point, and one that corroborates the rate card rather than contradicting it. Their Trustpilot profile exists and carries no reviews at all.

Now set that beside the agencies with substantial public records. The ones we can verify with large counts — Canopy Management at 4.9 across 35 Clutch reviews, Thrive at 4.6 across 109 — publish no rate card of their own, and in Thrive's case the ranges you will find are industry commentary rather than their prices. And Full Circle has neither a rate card nor a public review programme, so we are the least checkable firm in this comparison on both axes at once.

The pattern is worth stating because it changes how you shop. Transparency in this category comes in two different currencies, and firms rarely spend both. A published price makes a firm comparable before the call. A large review record makes it checkable after. Neither substitutes for the other, and an absence of one is not evidence of anything about the work — an empty Clutch profile means a firm has not run a review-solicitation programme, not that its clients are unhappy.

So do not read Darkroom's empty profiles as a warning, and do not read a competitor's 4.9 as a price. Ask each firm for the currency it does not publish: from Darkroom, three references at your scale who started in the last twelve months; from a well-reviewed firm that quotes, a written fee structure with the variables named. Ask us for both, since we publish neither.

What a starts-at figure does not tell you

A starting price answers one question — is this firm in my range at all — and leaves five open. Get these in writing before you compare it with any other quote.

  • Scope at that tier. How many ASINs, how many marketplaces, how many campaigns, and what happens when the catalogue grows or a second brand arrives under the same account.
  • Whether a percentage of media sits on top. Percentage arrangements are entirely normal here — we charge one in one of our products — but a fee that moves with your spend has to be visible before you sign rather than discovered in month four. At $100,000 a month of media, one undisclosed point is $12,000 a year.
  • Whether creative is inside it. On a per-service rate card, creative is its own line by design. If your advertising needs new video, that is a second retainer, not a rounding error.
  • Term and notice. Monthly rolling, quarterly or annual, how much notice each side gives, and whether the agreement renews automatically.
  • What makes it rise. New marketplaces, new brands, DSP being switched on, seasonal surges. Ask for the fee at today's scope and at double it, in the same message.

The reason a headline fee misleads is that the models behave differently as you grow. A flat retainer is expensive at low spend and cheap at high spend. A percentage is the reverse. A capped percentage behaves like one and then the other. Reading a rate card that prices each service separately

A per-service card is honest, but it shifts a decision onto you that a bundled quote hides: which lines you actually need. Three brands with the same revenue can land at very different totals.

  • Amazon only. One line, at the published floor. This is the comparison most readers of this page are running, and it is where a published figure is most directly useful.
  • Amazon plus creative. Two lines — Amazon management at $6,000/mo plus performance creative at $8,000/mo is $14,000 a month at the published floors, before scope. Worth knowing before the call rather than after, particularly if your ads are losing on the asset rather than the bid.
  • Amazon plus another storefront. TikTok Shop and retail media are separate lines again, and they should be — different platforms, usually different people.

Set against that, a bundled full-service quote can be cheaper for a brand that needs three or four functions, because the shared account overhead is only paid once. It is more expensive for a brand that needs one. Neither model is generous or mean; they are priced for different buyers. The way to tell which you are is to list the functions you would genuinely use in the next six months, then price both ways and compare totals rather than headline rates.

Five questions that make two Amazon quotes comparable, whoever they are from:

  • What would I pay this month, and what would I pay if spend doubled?
  • What exactly is excluded? The usual candidates are creative production, DSP management, additional marketplaces, and anything described as a project.
  • Is any software licence billed separately, and to whom? Some agencies pass tool costs through, some absorb them, some build their own.
  • What is the notice period, and what leaves with me?
  • Who is on the account for that fee, and how many other brands do they carry? Price without staffing is half a quote.

Two questions that decide more than the fee does

Every agency comparison ends up being about money, and the two things that most reliably determine whether the engagement works are not on any rate card. Both are from our own practice, and both apply to us as much as to anyone you are considering.

Ask which data they need from you, and by when. Across our managed accounts, the most common blocker on a profitability project is not strategy — it is that cost-of-goods data has been blank for four or five years on part of the catalogue. Without landed cost per product, every margin conversation is estimation and every pricing recommendation is a guess. Inventory positions are the second most withheld dataset. Any agency proposal that promises profit improvement without naming which data it needs from you, by when, is promising something it cannot deliver. That is a fair test to apply to us as readily as to Darkroom, and a firm that answers it precisely on the first call is telling you a great deal about how it works.

Ask what the timeline is, and whether they will put it in writing. We tell brands to expect four to six months to profitability on Amazon, faster if they already have direct-to-consumer traffic, with about a week to a skeleton listing and a month to a fully operational one. That is standing guidance we give prospects rather than a guarantee, and the gap between live and working is where most launch disappointment lives — reviews, conversion rate and placement compound over the first quarter and cannot be bought forward compliantly. Publishing that timeline costs us deals with brands who want a faster answer, and it is still the most useful thing to say to a brand that has been promised otherwise.

A monthly fee times six months is the real number you are approving. Compare that against the timeline each firm will commit to, not against the headline rate.

What to do with this

Before you compare subscription prices, pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders. On the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Whatever you buy — a seat, a service, or nothing — that number is the one it has to move, and a cheaper tool nobody has time to drive will not move it.

Common questions

How much does Darkroom cost for Amazon management?

Their pricing page lists Amazon management from $6,000/mo, alongside separate starting figures for TikTok Shop, retail media, paid media and creative. Because each service is priced separately, a brand wanting Amazon plus performance creative is looking at two lines. Read the current page before relying on any figure, and ask what scope the starting tier assumes.

Does Darkroom charge a percentage of ad spend?

Their pricing page states monthly starting prices and does not state a media percentage either way, so treat it as an open question rather than an answer. Ask directly whether a percentage applies above a spend threshold, at what rate, and on what basis. An undisclosed variable rate is the largest source of surprise in agency billing, whoever the agency is.

Is the $3,000 to $12,000 range Darkroom's price?

No, and that figure is not theirs at all — it comes from My Amazon Guy's published industry analysis, which describes what the market charges rather than what they charge. Agencies publish pricing guides that sit on pages looking exactly like pricing pages. Before printing any agency figure, check whether it is the price they charge or the price they say the market charges.

Does Darkroom have client reviews?

Read on 21 August 2026, their Clutch profile carries no client reviews and their Trustpilot profile carries none either. That is not evidence about the work — it means no review-solicitation programme has been run. Ask instead for three references at your scale who started in the last twelve months, which is a better test than a star rating anyway.

Why do so few Amazon agencies publish prices?

Because the work genuinely varies with catalogue size, marketplace count, creative needs and whether display is running, and a published number that fits nobody creates worse conversations than no number at all. It also costs a firm every enquiry below its floor and anchors every negotiation. It is a defensible position rather than a dodge — and it still costs the category comparability.

Is a flat monthly retainer better than a percentage of spend?

Neither is better in general; they favour different accounts. A flat fee is predictable and gets relatively cheaper as spend grows, which suits large budgets. A percentage starts smaller and scales with you, which suits growing ones, and a cap turns it into a flat fee above a threshold. The real question is which shape matches your next twelve months rather than your last three.
