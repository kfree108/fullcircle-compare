Amazon Agency Comparison: The Arithmetic Nobody Runs Before the Call

Updated 2026-08-21 · 2088 words · Written against what currently ranked for “amazon agency comparison”
The short answer

Amazon agency fees come in three shapes: a flat retainer, a percentage of ad spend, or a base plus a capped percentage. Each is cheapest inside a different band of spend, and the crossover points are simple arithmetic you can do in a spreadsheet before you speak to anyone.

Disclosure, and the rule this page follows

Full Circle is an Amazon agency and Dr. PPC is our product, so we are a party to this comparison and one of the numbers below is ours. Check the rest: every published figure here comes from a vendor's own pricing page, read on 20 August 2026, with the URLs at the foot.

One rule governs the whole page. We are not going to argue against percentage-of-spend pricing, because we charge it ourselves and so does the largest software vendor in this category. Arguments that a fee model is inherently wrong are almost always made by someone selling the other model. What is worth arguing is narrower and more useful: where each shape crosses over, and whether the percentage has a ceiling.

The three shapes, and who uses each

Flat retainer. A fixed monthly figure regardless of spend. Predictable, easy to budget, and the only shape most finance teams like on first reading. Darkroom publishes a per-service rate card with a starting figure for Amazon management, which is unusual in this category and a genuine advantage if you need a number before internal approval.

Percentage of ad spend. Widely used and often described as unusual by people who have not checked. It is how the category leader in software prices its top tier: Helium 10's Diamond plan includes 2% of managed ad spend on their own pricing page. That fact is worth knowing precisely because so few comparison articles mention it — percentage pricing is the norm, not an aberration.

Base plus percentage. A small fixed fee with a variable component on top. SellerApp's agency tier is published as $300 plus 0.5% to 2.5% of ad spend, which is close to the structure we use for Dr. PPC: $300 a month plus 3% of ad spend, capped, month to month, first 30 days free. Two firms arriving independently at nearly the same shape is a reasonable sign that the shape suits the work.

Programme phases. Worth naming as a fourth pattern because it breaks the arithmetic. Some firms sell a front-loaded diagnostic or launch phase followed by a lighter ongoing fee — AMZ Advisers describe an eight-week Global Blueprint phase before their international programme's ongoing phase. The headline number in that model is not the number that compounds, so price both.

The crossover arithmetic, worked

Two lines on a graph. A flat retainer is a horizontal line. A base-plus-percentage fee is a sloped line starting near zero. They cross once, and everything about which is cheaper depends on which side of that crossing your ad spend sits.

Take the two published structures above. A flat fee of six thousand dollars a month against a base of three hundred plus three per cent. Set them equal and solve, and the crossing sits at roughly one hundred and ninety thousand dollars of monthly ad spend — a level most brands never reach. Below it, the percentage structure is cheaper; above it, the flat fee is.

Except that is not the real answer, because of the cap. Dr. PPC's percentage stops at $2,500, which is reached at just over eighty-three thousand dollars of monthly spend. Past that point the fee is flat too — a maximum of $2,800 a month regardless of how large the account becomes. So the two lines never actually cross. That is what a cap does: it converts a variable fee into a flat one above a known threshold, and it removes the one genuine objection to percentage pricing, which is that an uncapped percentage keeps taking more from an account that is not necessarily getting more work.

Run the same arithmetic on any two quotes you receive. It takes four minutes, and it answers a question that no comparison article can answer for you, because the answer depends on your spend rather than on the vendors.

The variable nobody prices: the waste already in the account

Here is why fee comparison, done alone, is a poor use of a week.

From a named account we are cleared to discuss: on Walkize, $267,131 of ad spend produced $601,614 in attributed sales at a 44.4% ACOS. Within that, $89,885 — 33.6% of every dollar spent — went to search terms that produced zero orders, spread across 89,045 individual search terms, while the top 1% of terms carried 75.5% of all sales. The account ran 30,921 unique search terms and 244,510 clicks at a blended $1.09 cost per click. One account, one period, and not a portfolio claim.

Now compare magnitudes. The difference between two agency quotes on a mid-sized account is typically a few thousand dollars a month. The zero-order spend in that one account was nearly ninety thousand dollars over the period. If your account carries anything like that proportion, the fee is the second question and the first is which candidate can actually find it.

So do the export before you do the comparison. Ninety days of search-term data, one pivot, sum the spend where orders equal zero. Take that number into every conversation. It converts a vague brief into a specific one, and it lets you score candidates on whether they find your version of it unprompted.

Making structurally different quotes comparable

Fee shapes differ so much here that a like-for-like table is impossible until you normalise. Five steps:

  • Model at three spend levels, not one: your current spend, double it, and half. A structure that looks cheap today can be the expensive one after a good quarter, and the downside case matters just as much if you are seasonal.
  • Build a twenty-four-month total. Include setup fees, onboarding, any programme phase, creative charges and minimum terms. This is the only figure that survives structural differences.
  • Find the ceiling. If any part of the fee is a percentage, ask whether it is capped and at what level, and whether the cap survives a spend increase. An uncapped percentage is not wrong, but it is a different product from a capped one and should not be compared as though it were the same.
  • Check which billing basis you are reading. Monthly, annual, or annual-billed-monthly are three different numbers and pricing pages usually carry a toggle. Reading the wrong tab is the most common way a comparison goes wrong — check the tab on any vendor, ours included.
  • Divide by a denominator you both agree on. Attributed sales is the easy one and it is the weakest, because last-click attribution cannot prove incrementality and never could. If a candidate can propose a better denominator — a holdout, a matched control, a media-mix read — that proposal is worth more than a discount.

Which shape suits which brand

The shapes are not better or worse than each other. They fit different accounts.

  • A flat retainer suits high-spend accounts, brands with rigid budget approval processes, and anyone whose spend is about to rise sharply. Above the crossover point it is straightforwardly cheaper, and predictability has value of its own.
  • A percentage suits accounts that are small now, seasonal, or uncertain — the fee scales down when you do, which a flat retainer does not. It is also the shape most likely to keep a vendor engaged during a quiet quarter.
  • A capped base-plus-percentage suits the wide middle: brands that want the downside protection of a percentage and the ceiling of a flat fee. This is why our own product is built that way.
  • A programme with phases suits projects with a genuine front-loaded workload — an international launch, a catalogue rebuild, a migration between 1P and 3P. Price both phases before signing the first.

Whichever shape you choose, the firm matters more than the shape. Which one you should actually pick

A flat retainer is cheapest above the crossover point and suits high or rising spend. A percentage suits small, seasonal or uncertain accounts because it falls when you do. A capped base-plus-percentage suits the middle by giving you both a floor and a ceiling. Run the crossover arithmetic yourself in four minutes — then spend the rest of the week on the waste already in the account.

What to do with this

Shortlist on the job, not the feature grid. Pull your search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — 33.6% on the account above. Then ask each vendor on your list what they would do about it in week one, and see who answers with a process rather than a screenshot.

Common questions

Is percentage-of-ad-spend pricing normal for Amazon agencies?

Yes. It is how the largest software vendor in the category prices its top tier — Helium 10's Diamond plan includes 2% of managed ad spend on their own pricing page — and it appears widely on the agency side too. We charge a percentage ourselves. The question worth asking is not whether the model is used but whether the percentage has a ceiling.

At what ad spend does a flat retainer become cheaper?

Set the two fees equal and solve. A flat monthly fee equals a base-plus-percentage fee at the spend where the base plus the percentage of spend reaches the flat figure. If the percentage is capped, the two lines may never cross at all, because above the cap the variable fee becomes flat as well. Four minutes in a spreadsheet settles it.

How do I compare two Amazon agency quotes with different structures?

Model each at three spend levels — current, double and half — then build a twenty-four-month total including setup, onboarding, phase and creative charges. Confirm whether any percentage is capped and at what level. Check which billing tab you are reading on any pricing page, on every vendor including us. Then divide by a denominator you have both agreed on.

Do Amazon agencies publish their prices?

A minority do. Darkroom publishes a full per-service rate card. Most quote after a discovery call, which is the norm and ours too — Full Circle publishes no full-service rate, and neither Dr. DSP nor Dr. Stock has a published price. The only figure we publish is Dr. PPC's. On price transparency several competitors are genuinely ahead of us.

Should I choose an agency on fee at all?

It is the second question. Export ninety days of search-term data and total the spend on terms that produced zero orders. In most accounts that figure is larger than the difference between any two quotes on the shortlist, which means the deciding criterion is whether a candidate can find it — not what they charge to look.
